What Sponsored Display Actually Does

Sponsored Display is Amazon's audience-targeting and retargeting ad format. Instead of bidding on search keywords like Sponsored Products or Sponsored Brands, it shows your product to shoppers based on what they've browsed, viewed, or bought — on Amazon and on third-party sites and apps.

In 2024 Amazon folded Sponsored Display into a wider "Display ads" section of the console, alongside Amazon DSP. If you already run Sponsored Display campaigns, nothing changes — they keep serving under Campaign Manager exactly as before. If you're setting up something new, you'll find the same targeting options under "Display" when you create a campaign. The rename is a console reorganization, not a new product.

The core mechanic hasn't moved: you pick a targeting type, a bid model, and a budget, and Amazon shows your creative — either a template it generates automatically from your listing, or one you upload yourself — to shoppers who match.

Targeting: Product, Views, Purchases, and Interests

Sponsored Display has four targeting types, and eligibility differs by seller type. This is the detail most explainers skip, and it's the first thing to check if a campaign isn't spending.

  • Product targeting puts your ad on specific ASINs or categories — commonly a competitor's listing or a complementary product. Open to sellers and vendors enrolled in Brand Registry.
  • Views remarketing retargets shoppers who viewed your product page, or a similar one, in the last 30 days but didn't buy. Same eligibility as product targeting.
  • Purchases remarketing targets shoppers who bought your product or a related one — useful for consumables, replenishables, and accessories.
  • Amazon audiences / interests reaches shoppers based on lifestyle or category signals rather than direct product history. This one is vendor-only — a lot of seller-side confusion about missing interest targeting traces back to this rule.

Bidding and Budget: A Worked Example

Amazon's own defaults are a $1.00 default bid for CPC campaigns, $5.00 for vCPM, and a recommended daily budget of $40 to $60 for small and mid-sized advertisers. Those are starting points, not targets — here's what they mean in practice.

Say you run a $50 daily budget on a CPC campaign and, after a week of adjusting, your average cost per click settles around $0.75. That's roughly 66 clicks a day. If even a modest share of that traffic converts, you're looking at a handful of orders a day from display alone — before counting the sales that happen after a view with no click, which display is built to capture and search ads aren't.

Run the same $50 budget on vCPM at the $5.00 default instead, and you're buying viewable impressions rather than clicks — about 10,000 a day at that rate. That's the right model when the goal is reach and repetition, like a new launch or a seasonal push, rather than an immediate click.

The mistake we see most often here isn't the bid amount — it's picking CPC when the real goal is awareness, or vCPM when the real goal is direct response. Match the model to what you're actually trying to buy.

Sponsored Display Video and Where the Ads Actually Show Up

Sponsored Display video — sometimes called online video, or OLV — is a creative format inside Sponsored Display rather than a separate campaign type. You upload a video instead of relying on Amazon's auto-generated image creative, and it runs through the same product-targeting or audience-targeting setup as any other Sponsored Display campaign.

As for placement: on Amazon, that means product detail pages (often right below the buy box or customer reviews on a competitor's listing), search results, and Amazon-owned apps. Off Amazon, that means Twitch, Fire TV, Echo Show, and a range of third-party sites and apps through Amazon's ad exchange relationships. The exact placement follows the targeting type you picked — it's not something you choose directly.

If you've ever shopped for something, closed the tab, then seen that exact product follow you around a news site or a recipe blog, that's views remarketing in action. It's the most recognizable Sponsored Display format, and usually the first one sellers set up.

The Mistakes That Quietly Waste Spend

The three that cost the most, in order:

  • Leaving the default bid untouched. $1.00 CPC and $5.00 vCPM are starting points for the auction, not a recommendation. Left alone for weeks, they either overpay for low-value placements or underdeliver entirely.
  • Judging performance before the attribution window closes. Amazon can take 7 to 14 days to attribute a sale back to a display click or view. Pulling a campaign after three days because it "isn't converting" is a mistake we've made ourselves, more than once, early in an account.
  • Assuming Sponsored Display works like keyword search. There's no search term to negate, no match type to loosen. If a product-targeting campaign is underperforming, the fix is usually the ASIN list or the bid, not "better copy."

Whatever you change, know before you launch it what you're measuring and what would make you reverse it. That's true whether you're doing it by hand in Campaign Manager or automating it: every proposed change should carry the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger — otherwise you're just watching a number move and guessing why.

Where This Fits If You're Running More Than One Campaign Type

Sponsored Display rarely works well in isolation. It's most effective sitting alongside Sponsored Products for the click-and-convert layer and Sponsored Brands for top-of-search presence, with Display doing the retargeting and audience-expansion work in between. Managing bids across three ad types with different logic — keywords versus ASINs versus audiences — is usually where self-managed accounts start losing time, not money.

Which one you should actually pick

Self-managed Sponsored Display suits sellers with one or two SKUs and the patience to wait out the attribution window before judging results. Once Display is running alongside Sponsored Products and Brands across a real catalog, coordinating bids and targeting by hand gets slow — that's the point where automated or managed account management starts earning its cost.

Common questions

Is Sponsored Display being discontinued?

No. Amazon folded it into a broader "Display ads" section of the console in 2024, alongside Amazon DSP. Existing Sponsored Display campaigns keep running under Campaign Manager without interruption. New campaigns are created the same way, just under the "Display" label instead of the old name.

What's the difference between Sponsored Display and Sponsored Display video?

There isn't a separate program. Sponsored Display video, or OLV, is a creative format inside Sponsored Display — you upload video instead of using the auto-generated image creative, and it runs through the same product-targeting or audience-targeting setup as any other Sponsored Display campaign.

Do I need Brand Registry to run Sponsored Display?

Sellers need Brand Registry enrollment; vendors are eligible by default. One targeting option — Amazon audiences / interests — is vendor-only, which is the usual reason a seller can't find it in their console.

What's a good starting budget for Sponsored Display?

There's no minimum spend requirement. Amazon's own guidance for small and mid-sized advertisers is a daily budget of $40 to $60, with default bids of $1.00 for CPC and $5.00 for vCPM as a starting point to adjust from, not a target to hold.

Are Sponsored Display ads worth it?

As a complement to Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands, generally yes — it's the only Amazon ad type built specifically for retargeting shoppers who already looked at your listing. As a standalone strategy with no search-based campaigns underneath it, it's usually the wrong place to put your first ad dollar.
