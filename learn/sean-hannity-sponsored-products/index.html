What Sean Hannity has actually endorsed

Sean Hannity's most documented brand partnership is with Byrna, the non-lethal self-defense device maker. Byrna's own investor press release describes a 2023 partnership built on Hannity's radio show, his social following, and a dedicated "Hannity Discount Program" at byrna.com/hannity — not an Amazon listing, an Amazon ad, or anything running through Amazon's ad console.

Beyond Byrna, an endorsement-tracking database lists three more names tied to Hannity: Goldco, My Patriot Supply, and Roundhouse Provisions, with deals dated across 2024 and early 2025. That's third-party aggregated data, not a filing or a press release, so treat the exact dates and categories as directional rather than confirmed fact.

None of these four are Amazon Sponsored Products campaigns. They're personal endorsement deals — a public figure lending his name, audience, and airtime to a brand, in exchange for compensation the public releases don't disclose. That distinction matters, because "sponsored products" also happens to be the literal name of an Amazon ad format, and the two get confused constantly.

What 'Sponsored Products' actually means on Amazon

Amazon Sponsored Products is a pay-per-click ad format. A seller bids on keywords or products, Amazon inserts the ad into search results or onto competing product pages, and the seller pays only when someone clicks. There's no celebrity, no contract, no discount code tied to a person's name — it's an auction, running continuously, adjustable by the minute.

That's the opposite of what Hannity does for Byrna. His mentions run on radio and social media, point to a URL Byrna controls, and get measured by traffic and discount-code redemptions — not by Amazon's advertising console. A brand could have zero Sponsored Products spend and still run a celebrity partnership exactly like this one, and plenty do.

The confusion is understandable. Someone searching "sean hannity sponsored products" is likely combining two unrelated ideas — Hannity's paid endorsements, and the Amazon ad term — because both use the word "sponsored." They're separate systems that happen to share a word, nothing more.

Where the two actually intersect — and where they don't

They can intersect for a brand that sells on Amazon and also gets celebrity media exposure, but the intersection isn't automatic. Say a product gets mentioned on a syndicated radio show. Branded search volume for that product name might jump from a baseline of a few hundred searches a month to several thousand in the week of the mention. If the brand's Amazon Sponsored Products campaigns only target generic category keywords, none of that jump gets captured — Sponsored Products bids on keywords and ASINs, not on media appearances, and it can't "hear" a radio mention happen.

To catch that demand, the brand needs a branded exact-match campaign live and adequately funded before the mention airs, plus enough inventory to cover the spike. Byrna's approach — sending Hannity's audience straight to a dedicated page on its own site — sidesteps this problem entirely, because it controls that landing page completely. A brand relying on Amazon as the point of sale doesn't get that luxury; it's competing for its own branded search term against anyone else running ads on it, including resellers.

The common mistake: crediting the wrong channel

The mistake we see most often — and one we've made ourselves early in an account — is watching a sales spike after an earned-media hit and assuming the Sponsored Products campaign caused it, then pulling budget from other campaigns to "double down" on what's working. Nothing in the ad account actually changed. The mention did the work; the ad console just happened to be open at the same time.

The fix is to check attribution before acting on it. Pull the search term report for the week of the spike. If clicks are landing on a branded exact-match term that already existed and was already funded, the ads may genuinely have caught real demand. If the spike shows up in organic sessions and total branded search volume with no change in ad spend or bids, the ads didn't cause it — the mention did — and crediting the ad account leads to the wrong decision next month. Every proposed change to an account should carry the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger, precisely so this kind of misattribution gets caught before budget moves on the strength of a coincidence.

What to do if the numbers don't back up the story

If you've checked and the lift isn't real — ACOS looks the same as last month, TACOS hasn't moved, branded impressions are flat — don't quietly leave the extra budget in place hoping it starts working. Roll it back to where it was before the assumed spike, and treat the endorsement as a top-of-funnel event you currently can't measure inside Amazon's ad console, not a PPC win.

If the setting that caused the confusion is already live — budget increased, bids raised, a new campaign launched off the back of a spike that's since faded — that's a normal thing to catch and reverse. It's not a sign the account is broken. It's a sign the measurement step got skipped, and that's fixable the next time a mention happens.

Which one you should actually pick

Endorsement deals like Hannity's suit brands that already control their own site and want reach beyond Amazon, on terms Amazon's ad console can't measure. Sponsored Products suits brands whose buyers complete the purchase on Amazon and who need attribution inside that platform. The real risk isn't picking the wrong channel — it's crediting one for the other's result.

Common questions

Is Sean Hannity's Byrna partnership an Amazon Sponsored Products deal?

No. Byrna's own press release describes a radio and media partnership pointing to byrna.com/hannity with dedicated discount codes. There's no mention of an Amazon listing or Amazon ad campaign anywhere in the announcement.

What other brands has Sean Hannity endorsed?

An endorsement-tracking database also lists Goldco, My Patriot Supply, and Roundhouse Provisions, with deals dated across 2024 and early 2025. That's third-party aggregated data rather than a company filing, so treat specifics as directional.

What is Amazon Sponsored Products, exactly?

It's a cost-per-click ad format sellers use on Amazon: you bid on keywords or products, Amazon places the ad in search results or on competing listings, and you pay per click, not per impression.

Can I use Sponsored Products to capture demand from a celebrity mention?

Only if your product is actually sold on Amazon and you have a funded branded exact-match campaign live before the mention airs. Generic category targeting won't reliably catch a spike tied to a specific media appearance.

Why did my Sponsored Products numbers look great right after a media mention, then flatten?

That's usually the media halo fading, not the ad account losing effectiveness. Check whether the lift showed up in organic branded search too — if it did, the ads were riding a wave they didn't create.
