What the Helium 10 PPC Audit Actually Checks

The audit runs five checks against your connected Amazon account: PPC Analysis (search terms getting clicks but no sales), Keyword Opportunities (terms competitors rank for that you don't bid on), Listing Health (a score for missing bullets, images, or other conversion blockers), Keyword Analysis (how the keywords you already bid on are performing), and Duplicate Keyword Analysis (cases where you're bidding against yourself across campaigns).

It requires a connected PPC token to show any advertising data — without it, you still get a report, just not the PPC half. It's free to run and it's explicitly read-only: nothing in your campaigns changes until a human opens the relevant tool (Ads, Black Box, Cerebro) and acts on the recommendation.

That's the honest scope of a Helium 10 PPC audit. It's a diagnostic snapshot, not a management system. Worth knowing before you run it: the value is entirely in what you do with the list it hands you.

A Worked Example: Reading the Output

Say your account spends $8,000 a month and your audit flags a search term with 340 clicks and 0 orders, sitting inside an exact-match campaign at a $1.40 bid. That's roughly $475 spent for nothing over the lookback window — a legitimate flag. But the audit report stops there. It won't tell you whether that term is new (and needs more data before you judge it) or has been dead for six months (and should have been cut already).

Same with Listing Health: a score of 62/100 on your top ASIN might be driven by two missing lifestyle images, which is a five-minute fix, or by a thin bullet structure, which needs a rewrite and a testing period before you'll see any ranking movement. The score tells you there's a problem. It doesn't tell you the size of the fix or what it's worth doing first.

This is where most sellers get stuck — not reading the report, but prioritizing it against everything else competing for their time.

What the Audit Won't Do For You

Because it's read-only, the audit has no mechanism to weigh a recommendation against your actual account economics. It flags a wasted search term the same way whether cutting it saves you $50 a month or $5,000. It doesn't know your margin by SKU, so it can't tell you which fixes actually move profit versus which just look bad in a report.

It also treats each flag independently. It won't tell you that pausing keyword A will shift spend onto keyword B at a worse ACoS, because it isn't watching what happens after you act — there's no before/after measurement built in, and no way to undo a change that backfires.

That gap is the whole difference between an audit and ongoing management. Every proposed change carries three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. A one-time report can give you the first. It can't give you the second or third, because it isn't there to watch what happens next.

When the Audit Finds Bad News — What To Do Next

The most common bad-news finding is a search term burning spend with no sales. Before you pause it, check the sample size. Thirty clicks and no sales on a $0.60 keyword isn't evidence of anything yet — that's a mistake we've made ourselves early in an account, cutting a term that just hadn't had enough impressions to convert once. Give it a real sample relative to your category's typical conversion rate before you act on it.

Duplicate Keyword Analysis is the other one that gets misread. Bidding on the same term in both an auto and a manual campaign isn't always waste — sometimes it's deliberate, to defend a branded term or a competitor ASIN target from being outbid. Don't prune every duplicate the report shows you; check what each campaign is actually defending first.

If a Listing Health fix doesn't move anything after you make it, don't assume the audit was wrong. Check whether you gave it enough time and traffic to actually register — image and copy changes take longer to show up in conversion rate than a bid change does.

Common Mistakes Sellers Make With a PPC Audit

  • Running it once and never again. An audit is a snapshot. Account performance shifts weekly; a report from two months ago is describing an account that no longer exists.
  • Treating the ranked list as a to-do list in order. The audit ranks by impact estimate, not by what's actually costing you the most margin right now. Cross-check against your own P&L before you start at the top.
  • Disconnecting the PPC token and not noticing. Without it reconnected, the report quietly stops showing advertising data and you're auditing a listing, not a campaign.
  • Acting on small-sample flags. Covered above, but worth repeating — it's the single most common way sellers cut a keyword that would have converted with two more weeks of data.

Common questions

Is the Helium 10 PPC audit free?

Yes. It's a free tool inside Helium 10 that reads your connected Amazon account and generates a ranked list of opportunities. You don't need a paid subscription to run it, though acting on some recommendations routes you into paid Helium 10 tools like Ads or Cerebro.

Does the audit change my campaigns automatically?

No. It's explicitly read-only. It surfaces recommendations — pause this term, raise that budget, fix this listing gap — but every change has to be made by hand. Nothing runs on its own.

Why don't I see PPC insights in my audit report?

PPC insights only appear if your PPC token is connected to Helium 10. Without that connection, you'll still get a report covering listing health and keyword opportunities, just not campaign-level advertising data.

How often should I run a Helium 10 PPC audit?

Monthly is a reasonable floor for most accounts, more often if you're actively launching or running promotions. A single audit is a snapshot of that day's account; spend and search behavior move fast enough that a quarter-old report is describing a different account.

What's the difference between a PPC audit and PPC management?

An audit tells you what's wrong right now. Management is the ongoing work of acting on that, watching what happens after each change, and adjusting when it doesn't work. A free audit tool can do the first. It has no mechanism for the second.
