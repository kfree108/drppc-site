What Are Amazon Sponsored Products, and How Do They Actually Work?

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1700 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon sponsored products”
The short answer

Amazon Sponsored Products are pay-per-click ads that promote individual product listings in search results and on product pages. You pick products and keywords, or let Amazon match them automatically, set a bid and budget, and pay only when a shopper clicks.

What Sponsored Products Actually Do

Sponsored Products puts one listing in front of a shopper who is already searching or browsing for something like it. The ad looks almost identical to an organic listing — same image, same price, a small "Sponsored" label — and it drops the shopper straight onto that product's detail page. There's no landing page to build and no creative to design; the product listing itself is the ad.

It's open to Professional sellers, vendors, book vendors, Kindle Direct Publishing authors, and agencies acting on their behalf. The product has to have inventory and be eligible to win the Featured Offer (what used to be called the Buy Box) — an ad won't run for an out-of-stock item or one buried behind a suppressed listing, no matter how much you bid.

Placement is broader than most sellers assume. Ads show at the top, middle, and bottom of search results and on competing product pages on Amazon, and Amazon also places them on select third-party sites and apps outside Amazon. It's still one ad type, one bidding mechanism — the placement just moves depending on where Amazon thinks the click is most likely to convert.

How Targeting and the Auction Work

Every Sponsored Products campaign runs on a bid: the maximum you'll pay when someone clicks. You're not paying for impressions or guaranteed placement — you're entering an auction against other sellers targeting the same search term or product, and Amazon's system weighs your bid against relevance and past performance to decide who shows where.

You choose how that matching happens. Automatic targeting lets Amazon read your listing and decide which search terms and products to match against — fastest to launch, useful for a brand-new ASIN with no search history to build keywords from. Manual targeting means you pick the keywords or the specific ASINs yourself, with tighter control over where spend goes but more work to set up and maintain.

A Worked Example: Turning a Daily Budget Into Orders

Numbers make this concrete faster than definitions do. Say you set a $20 daily budget with an average cost-per-click of $0.75. That buys roughly 26 clicks a day. If 10% of those clicks convert, that's about 2.6 orders. At a $30 average sale price, that's roughly $78 in ad-attributed revenue against $20 of spend — an ACOS (ad cost as a percentage of ad sales) of about 25.6%.

Whether that 25.6% is good or bad depends entirely on your margin, not on some universal benchmark. A product with 40% margin can carry that ACOS and still profit. A product with 15% margin can't — the ad is losing money even though it's technically converting. This is the calculation people skip: they look at ACOS in isolation instead of against their own product economics, and either kill a profitable campaign too early or keep funding a loss because the sales number looks encouraging.

How to Get Sponsored Products Live on Your Account

You register for advertising through your Seller Central, Vendor Central, Author Central, or KDP account — there's a separate signup if you're advertising products sold outside Amazon. Once registered, you create a campaign, choose Sponsored Products, add the ASINs you want to advertise, pick automatic or manual targeting, and set a daily budget.

Amazon publishes minimum daily budgets by marketplace — $10 USD in the US, for example, with different minimums in local currency elsewhere. There's no signup fee and no monthly platform charge from Amazon itself; you're billed on the clicks you get, at whatever your bid settles to in the auction.

  • Eligibility check first: confirm the ASIN has inventory and is winning the Featured Offer before you build the campaign around it.
  • Start with 25-30+ keywords if you're going manual, or launch automatic first to harvest real search terms before narrowing.
  • Leave campaigns running without an end date so Amazon keeps refreshing auto-targeting matches against current shopping trends.

The Mistakes That Quietly Burn Budget

The most common one: leaving a campaign on automatic targeting indefinitely and never mining the search term report for the keywords that are actually converting. Automatic is a fine way to start; it's a poor way to stay, because it doesn't let you cut the terms that click without buying.

The second is judging a campaign inside the first 3-5 days. Conversion has lag — a click today can convert three days later — and killing a campaign on day three because ACOS looks ugly is a mistake we've made ourselves managing other people's accounts, usually under pressure to show a number by Friday. Give it at least a full week, longer for anything with a considered purchase cycle.

The third is treating negative targeting as optional. Search terms that generate clicks but never convert are draining budget from terms that do. Reviewing and excluding them isn't a nice-to-have step — it's the difference between a campaign that improves over time and one that plateaus.

When the Numbers Look Bad

If ACOS is high and impressions are low, the bid is probably too low to win the auction — raising it, even slightly, often does more than a targeting overhaul. If impressions are healthy but clicks are scarce, the problem is usually the listing itself: main image, price, or review count relative to what's ranking above you. If clicks are strong but conversion is weak, the ad is doing its job and the product page isn't closing the sale — that's a listing fix, not an ads fix.

If a keyword or ASIN target has burned through 15-20 clicks with zero conversions, it's a negative candidate, not a patience test. Waiting longer on a target with real click volume and no conversions rarely turns into a good outcome.

Common questions

How much do Amazon Sponsored Products cost?

There's no signup fee or monthly platform charge from Amazon for running Sponsored Products yourself — you set a maximum bid and pay only when a shopper clicks. Daily budget minimums vary by marketplace, for example $10 USD in the US. If you use a managed service or software layered on top, check that vendor's current pricing directly, since those are separate charges from Amazon's own click cost.

Who is eligible to run Sponsored Products?

Professional sellers, vendors, book vendors, Kindle Direct Publishing authors, and agencies acting on their behalf. The product itself also needs to be in an eligible category and able to win the Featured Offer — an ad won't serve for an out-of-stock or suppressed listing regardless of bid.

What's the difference between Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands?

Sponsored Products promotes a single product listing and sends the click to that product's detail page. Sponsored Brands promotes the brand itself — a custom headline, logo, and a handful of products — and typically sends the click to a Brand Store or a curated landing page rather than a single listing.

Do I need Brand Registry to run Sponsored Products?

No. Sponsored Products is open to any eligible seller, vendor, or author account. Brand Registry unlocks other things, like A+ Content on the listing and access to Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display, but it's not a requirement for Sponsored Products itself.

How long before Sponsored Products start working?

Impressions and clicks usually show up within a day or two of launch. Conversion data needs longer — give a new campaign at least a full week before judging ACOS, since clicks often convert with a lag of several days rather than instantly.
