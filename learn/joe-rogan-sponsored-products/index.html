Who's actually on the sponsored list

The Joe Rogan Experience runs a rotating roster of paid ad reads, not a fixed sponsor deck. The categories repeat: supplements (AG1, Onnit, Four Sigmatic), coffee (Black Rifle Coffee), grooming (Manscaped, Dr. Squatch), sleep tech (Eight Sleep), online privacy (ExpressVPN), mental health (BetterHelp), and cookware — HexClad has been read on the show and sells on Amazon, which makes it a useful example of how this works in practice.

The lists that rank for this query today mix currently-live sponsors with reads that stopped years ago. A promo code with a 2020 or 2023 "last ad" date next to it is a historical record, not a live offer. If you're trying to confirm whether a brand is actively sponsoring right now, the date matters more than the presence of the logo.

None of this changes if the brand also sells on Amazon. What changes is what an Amazon seller should do about it, and almost nothing written about JRE sponsors covers that part.

Two different things get called "sponsored products"

This is the gap in almost every page ranking for this search. "Joe Rogan sponsored products" (the podcast ad reads) and "Sponsored Products" (Amazon's own PPC ad format, capital letters, inside Seller Central) are unrelated things that happen to share a name. The confusion is common because both are, technically, paid product endorsement.

The connection between them is real, though — it's just indirect. A JRE ad read is an off-Amazon demand trigger. Amazon Sponsored Products is the on-Amazon mechanism that either captures that demand or lets a competitor capture it instead. Listeners who hear "HexClad" on a podcast open Amazon and type the brand name into the search bar within the hour. What they see next — your listing's ad, a competitor's ad, or nothing — is decided by bids that were set before the episode dropped, not after.

What happens on Amazon after the ad airs

Here's the mechanism, worked through with round numbers to show the shape of it — not a reported result, just the math of how a branded spike behaves.

Say a cookware brand runs a modest Sponsored Products budget on its own brand term, sized for an average day. The episode drops at 7am, Rogan reads the ad at the 40-minute mark, and by 9am branded search volume for that name is running several multiples of baseline. If the daily budget was set for baseline traffic, it exhausts by mid-morning — and for the rest of the day, anyone searching the brand name sees a competitor's ad in the top slot instead, because competitors can and do bid on brand terms that start trending.

The fix isn't a bigger permanent budget. It's a budget that scales for a known event and comes back down afterward. That's the difference between a spike you catch and a spike you fund forever by accident.

The common mistake — including one we've made

The mistake most brands make is sizing the budget off the trailing seven-day average, because that's the number the dashboard shows by default. A JRE drop-day spike doesn't show up in a trailing average until it's already over.

The mistake we've made ourselves: assuming the spike lands in the first two hours. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the episode doesn't get clipped and shared until day two, and the real search bump shows up 30 hours later, after the initial budget bump has already been switched off. The lesson wasn't "budget more" — it was to check the search term report daily for a few days after any known media event, not just on the day itself.

  • Don't raise budget and forget to lower it — a permanent 5x budget on a term that's back to baseline just burns spend on branded searches you'd have won anyway.
  • Don't assume day one is the only spike day — clips and re-uploads extend the window unpredictably.
  • Do treat any budget change tied to a media event as a hypothesis, not a set-and-forget setting: know why you're making the change, how you'll know it worked, and what triggers you to reverse it. Every proposed change carries three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger — that discipline matters more here than the size of the bid.

If you already missed the spike

Bad news first: if the budget capped out and you didn't notice for two days, that traffic is gone. There's no retroactive fix. What you can do is make sure it doesn't happen the next time the brand gets mentioned anywhere.

Pull the search term report for the 48 hours after the air date. If branded search volume is up sharply and your ad's impression share dropped mid-day, that's the budget cap, confirmed. Check whether a competitor's ASIN shows up on your own brand term during that window — if it does, that's the seat you lost. Raise the daily cap on the branded campaign going forward, set a calendar reminder ahead of any known sponsorship, and pull the budget back down once search volume returns to baseline. That last step is the one people forget.

Common questions

Does Joe Rogan personally endorse these products or is it just a paid read?

It's a paid ad read, sometimes accompanied by Rogan describing genuine personal use of the product. The two aren't mutually exclusive — some reads are contractual sponsorships where he also says he uses the item daily. Either way, it's advertising, and the brand paid for the placement.

Is HexClad still a current JRE sponsor?

The public sponsor lists that rank for this query show a range of "last aired" dates, and those pages get updated at different rates, so treat any single list as a snapshot rather than a live feed. If it matters for your purposes, check the date attached to the specific entry rather than trusting inclusion on the list alone.

Should an Amazon seller bid on "joe rogan" as a keyword?

Usually not directly — that phrase rarely carries buying intent on its own. What matters is defending your own brand name and close variants during the window after a mention airs, since that's the term shoppers actually type when they go looking for the product they just heard about.

How long does the traffic bump from a podcast mention actually last?

It varies by episode and by whether clips get reshared, but the general shape is a sharp rise in the first day or two followed by a decay back toward baseline over roughly a week. Promo codes with no expiration date can extend the tail; codes that quietly stop working end it faster.

What's the actual difference between a JRE sponsorship and Amazon Sponsored Products?

A JRE sponsorship is an off-Amazon media buy — you pay for airtime on a podcast. Amazon Sponsored Products is an on-Amazon auction-based ad format you run inside Seller Central or Vendor Central. They're unrelated products that share a name; the only real link is that one can create a demand spike the other has to be ready to catch.
