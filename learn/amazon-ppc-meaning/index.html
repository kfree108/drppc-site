What Amazon PPC Actually Means

PPC stands for pay-per-click. On Amazon, it means you set up an ad, pick keywords or products to target, and Amazon shows your ad in search results or on product pages. You pay nothing for the impression — the view. You pay only when someone clicks. That's the entire meaning of the term, and it applies whether you're running Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, or Sponsored Display.

The confusion usually isn't the word itself — it's that "PPC" gets used two ways. Sometimes it means the billing model (pay per click, as opposed to pay per thousand impressions, or CPM). Sometimes it's shorthand for the whole practice — "doing PPC" — meaning running and managing Amazon's ad campaigns generally. Both usages are correct. Context tells you which one a person means.

One hard gate worth knowing early: Amazon PPC only works on a Professional selling plan. Individual sellers can't access Sponsored Products, Brands, or Display at all — that's an account-level restriction, not a setting hiding somewhere in the console.

How the Auction Sets Your Cost — A Worked Example

Amazon PPC runs on a second-price-style auction, adjusted for relevance. You don't necessarily pay your bid — you typically pay just above whoever bid below you, provided your ad is relevant enough to win the slot at all.

Say you bid $1.20 on a keyword. The next-highest relevant bidder is at $0.95. You win the placement and Amazon charges you around $0.96 for that click, not your full $1.20. That $0.96 is your CPC (cost per click) for the day on that keyword. Now say it takes 20 clicks at $0.96 to land one $32 sale. Your ad spend for that sale is $19.20 — an ACOS (advertising cost of sale) of 60%. Neither number is good or bad in isolation. It depends entirely on your margin on that $32 product. That's the piece most definitions skip: PPC is a bidding mechanism, not a spend target. The dollar figure only means something next to your actual margin.

In a competitive category — cookware is a fair example, which is one reason a brand like HexClad runs PPC continuously rather than seasonally — CPCs sit higher because more sellers are bidding on the same small set of high-intent keywords.

What "Amazon PPC Specialist" Means

An Amazon PPC specialist is the person — in-house, freelance, or agency-side — who runs the account day to day: keyword research, bid changes, negative keywords, budget pacing, and reading the search term report to catch wasted spend before it compounds. The title just means someone who works Amazon's ad console specifically, rather than a generalist who splits time across Google, Meta, and Amazon.

What a specialist actually does most days is unglamorous: check yesterday's spend against target ACOS, mine the search term report for keywords showing up in the wrong campaign, nudge bids up on winners and down on high-spend/no-sale terms, and add negative keywords so a $40 product stops paying for clicks meant for a $15 one. The job title implies more strategy than most days deliver — a lot of the work is pattern-matching against last week's numbers.

The Common Mistake — And What to Do When the Number Looks Wrong

The most common mistake — one we've made too — is reacting to a single bad day's ACOS by cutting bids across the board. Amazon's attribution window means a sale can post days after the click. A Tuesday that looks terrible sometimes fixes itself by Thursday once attributed sales catch up. Cutting bids on that first bad signal often kills the exact campaigns that were about to convert.

A second common mistake is optimizing Sponsored Products in isolation and ignoring what it's doing to organic rank. A keyword that looks unprofitable in PPC alone can be earning its keep by pushing organic placement — a lift the ACOS number never shows.

When a number looks wrong — CPC higher than expected, ACOS spiking, conversions at zero — check three things before touching a bid: the date range (is this really today, or a lag artifact), the search term report (is spend landing on the keyword you targeted, or a broad-match tangent), and whether price or inventory changed underneath the campaign without anyone telling ads. Most "PPC is broken" moments are a listing or data-lag problem wearing a PPC costume. It's also the discipline we hold every automated change to: every proposed change carries three things before it runs — the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. That standard isn't specific to software. Any specialist should be able to tell you why they moved a bid, what they expected to happen, and what they'll do if it doesn't.

Common questions

Is PPC the same as CPC on Amazon?

No. PPC (pay-per-click) is the billing model — you're charged per click, not per impression. CPC (cost per click) is the actual dollar figure you pay for that click. Amazon's own materials use the two interchangeably in casual writing, but PPC is the system and CPC is the metric it produces.

How much does Amazon PPC cost?

There's no fixed price. Cost per click is set by auction and can range from a few cents to several dollars, depending on how competitive your category and keywords are. A more useful planning number is a percentage of revenue — commonly discussed in the 10–15% range — then adjusted against your own margin rather than a generic benchmark.

Can I run Amazon PPC on an Individual seller account?

No. Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display all require a Professional selling plan. This is an account-level restriction, not a campaign setting, so upgrading the plan is the only fix.

What's a good ACOS for Amazon PPC?

There isn't one universal number — it depends on your margin. A 30% ACOS is comfortable on a product with a 55% margin and a real problem on one with a 20% margin. Compare your ACOS to your own break-even point (roughly your margin before ad spend), not to an industry rule of thumb.

What does an Amazon PPC specialist do day to day?

Mostly reactive work: bid adjustments based on yesterday's data, search term report review, negative keyword additions, budget pacing so campaigns don't run out mid-day, and occasional campaign restructuring. The title sounds strategic; the daily reality is closer to careful, repeated pattern-matching.
