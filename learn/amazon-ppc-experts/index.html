The short answer

An Amazon PPC expert manages bids, keywords, and budgets across Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display to lower ACOS or grow revenue at a target ACOS. They're hired as freelancers, agencies, or in-house hires — pricing runs hourly, per-project, or as a retainer plus a percentage of spend.

What the job actually involves

The job is not "turn campaigns on and check back next month." A working Amazon PPC expert is in the account weekly, sometimes daily, because Amazon's auction and your competitors' bids move constantly. The core loop is: read the search term report, find which keywords are spending without converting, cut or lower them, find which ones are converting under target ACOS, raise them, and repeat.

Underneath that loop sits structural work most listings don't mention: campaign architecture (so Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display aren't bidding against each other), negative keyword hygiene, placement and dayparting adjustments, budget pacing so campaigns don't die at noon, and reconciling ad performance against actual unit economics — not just ACOS, but contribution margin after Amazon's fees.

  • Keyword harvesting — pulling converting search terms out of broad/auto campaigns into exact match
  • Negative keyword management — cutting spend on terms that will never convert
  • Bid and budget adjustment — moving money toward what's working, away from what isn't
  • Placement and dayparting review — top-of-search often costs more per click but converts differently than product page placement
  • Reporting against margin, not just ACOS — a low ACOS on a low-margin SKU can still lose money

Freelancer, agency, or in-house — and why so many listings are from Pakistan and India

Search "Amazon PPC expert" on any freelance marketplace and most of what comes back is individual freelancers and small agencies, heavily concentrated in Pakistan and India, priced per project rather than per hour — commonly in the low hundreds of dollars for a defined scope like an account audit or a campaign setup. That's a legitimate, fair channel. There are genuinely skilled specialists there, and a fixed-price audit or second opinion is a reasonable way to get one without committing to a retainer.

Where that model runs out of road is continuous management. PPC is not a one-off deliverable — it needs the weekly loop described above, and a per-project freelance engagement usually ends when the deliverable is marked complete, not when the account needs its next adjustment. Location doesn't determine skill; scope does. An Amazon PPC expert in India or anywhere else is worth exactly what their process produces, and a portfolio of five-star reviews on a marketplace tells you about the deliverable, not about what happens in week six.

The alternative to freelance is a retainer agency (monthly fee plus a percentage of spend) or an in-house hire (salary, benefits, ramp time). Both are more suited to an account that needs ongoing attention across a full catalog rather than a single fix.

How to tell if the work is real: a worked example

Say an account runs $8,000 a month in ad spend at 38% ACOS, against a 25% target. A real expert should be able to show you the specific keywords driving that gap — not just the aggregate number. If one keyword took 60 clicks at $2 each ($120 spend) with zero orders, that's the first $120 to cut. If a different keyword is converting at 12% ACOS and currently capped by a low bid, that's where the freed budget should go.

The math is not complicated. What's hard is doing it every week, on every campaign, without breaking what's already working. That's the actual skill being sold — not the concept of bid optimization, which anyone can define, but the discipline of checking, adjusting, and re-checking on a schedule.

Ask for exactly this before you hire: which keywords they'd cut in week one, which they'd raise, and how they'd know within two weeks whether the change worked. If the answer is vague — "we'll optimize based on performance" — that's not a plan, it's a placeholder.

The common mistakes — including ones we've made

The most common mistake is optimizing ACOS in isolation and starving reach — cutting every keyword that isn't converting today, including the ones building the search term data you'll need next month. A close second is chasing top-of-search placement bonuses without checking whether the incremental sales are actually incremental, or just cannibalizing organic rank you already had.

We've made this one ourselves: turning off a campaign that looked like dead weight on a two-week view, only to find it was feeding conversion data that kept a neighboring campaign's algorithm calibrated. That's exactly why every proposed change we run now carries three things before it goes live: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. If a change doesn't do what the evidence predicted, there's already a defined way back — not a scramble.

The other mistake, less talked about: treating PPC as the fix for a problem PPC can't fix. If the listing has a weak main image, a price above the competitive set, or a stockout risk, no amount of bid optimization moves the number. A good expert will tell you that before taking your budget, not after.

When the fix doesn't work

If ACOS hasn't moved in two to three weeks, the first question isn't "is the expert bad," it's "was there enough traffic to draw a conclusion." A keyword with 40 clicks a month can swing wildly on pure noise. Ask for the volume behind any claim before accepting the claim.

Second, check for confounds. Did the price change in that window? Did the listing go out of stock for three days? Did a competitor's price drop pull buy box away? PPC results get blamed for problems that started somewhere else in the account.

If the plan was sound, the volume was adequate, and nothing else changed, and it still didn't work — that's useful information, not a failure to hide. Ask what the rollback is, and what the next hypothesis is. An expert who can't answer either question wasn't measuring in the first place.

Common questions

What does an Amazon PPC expert cost?

It depends entirely on the model. Freelance marketplace listings for a defined project commonly run from around a hundred dollars up to several hundred. Agencies typically charge a monthly retainer plus a percentage of ad spend, and that percentage is often not published upfront — always ask for it directly, since on a large budget one percentage point is real money. Check any vendor's current pricing on their own page rather than a listicle; scraped or third-party prices are frequently out of date.

Is hiring an Amazon PPC expert on Fiverr worth it?

For a narrow, defined task — an account audit, a campaign setup, a second opinion on structure — yes, and there are genuinely skilled people on these platforms. For ongoing weekly management of a growing catalog, a per-project engagement usually isn't built to keep going once the deliverable is marked complete.

Are Amazon PPC experts in India or Pakistan any good?

Many are. A large share of the freelancers and small agencies offering Amazon PPC services on global marketplaces are based in India and Pakistan, and geography doesn't determine skill. What matters is the same regardless of location: can they show you the specific keyword-level evidence behind a recommendation, and do they have a measurement plan for whether it worked.

How long before I know if my Amazon PPC expert is working?

Give it at least two to three weeks of adequate traffic before drawing conclusions — Amazon's attribution has lag, and low-volume keywords swing on noise. Ask upfront what volume threshold they'll use before calling a change a success or failure.

Can I manage Amazon PPC myself instead of hiring an expert?

Yes, if you have a small catalog and the time to check the search term report and adjust bids weekly. As the catalog and spend grow, the time cost of doing it properly usually outpaces the cost of paying someone — freelance, agency, or otherwise — to do it.
