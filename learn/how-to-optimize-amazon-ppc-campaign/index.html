How to Optimize an Amazon PPC Campaign (With a Worked Example)

The short answer

Optimizing an Amazon PPC campaign means running a repeatable loop: read the search-term report, add negatives for wasted spend, raise bids on keywords that convert, lower or pause the ones that don't, then wait for enough clicks before judging again. It's ongoing, not a one-time setup.

The Four Levers You're Actually Pulling

Every Amazon PPC "optimization" is really just one of four moves: change a bid, change a keyword or target, add a negative, or change a budget. Dayparting, placement multipliers, campaign structure — all of it is a wrapper around those four. If you can't name which lever you're pulling on a given day, you're guessing.

The order matters. Fix negatives first — they stop money leaking on searches that will never convert. Then adjust bids on keywords with enough data to trust. Only then touch budget: raising a budget on a campaign with a bad ACOS just buys more of the same problem, faster.

  • Bids — what you're willing to pay per click, adjustable at the keyword, target, or placement level.
  • Keywords/targets — what you're bidding on, added from the search-term report once a term proves itself.
  • Negatives — what you're telling Amazon never to show your ad for.
  • Budget — how much oxygen the whole campaign gets; the multiplier on everything above.

A Worked Example: Turning a Search-Term Report Into a Decision

Say you're running a broad-match campaign on a $150 chef knife set, target ACOS 30%. Three weeks in, pull the search-term report. One exact-match keyword, "stainless steel chef knife set" at a $1.10 bid, has 40 clicks, $44 spent, and 3 orders worth $135 attributed sales. That's 32.6% ACOS — close enough to the target to leave the bid alone, not clearly bad enough to cut it.

A search term sitting under that broad match, "cheap knife set for kids", has 12 clicks, $9.60 spent, zero sales. Twelve clicks is enough to call it: that shopper isn't your buyer. It becomes a negative exact, not a maybe.

A third term, "damascus chef knife", only has 3 clicks. That's not a verdict either way — three clicks can look like 0% or 100% conversion depending on luck, and neither number means anything yet. The correct move is nothing: leave the bid, don't negative it, check again once it clears roughly 15–20 clicks.

As a working rule: wait for a keyword to clear about 10 clicks before deciding it deserves a negative, and 20–30 clicks before trusting its ACOS enough to move a bid meaningfully. Below that threshold you're optimizing noise, not performance.

When the Number Is Wrong, or the Fix Didn't Work

Sometimes the report is the problem, not the campaign. Attribution lags — a click today can convert three days later, so the "zero sales" you're staring at on Tuesday might read differently by Friday. Before you pause anything sitting right on the edge of your ACOS target, check whether the reporting window has actually closed.

Sometimes the fix you made simply doesn't work: you raised a bid and impressions didn't move, or you added a negative and ACOS didn't budge. That's information too — it usually means the lever you pulled wasn't the actual constraint. A bid increase won't fix a listing with a weak main image. A negative won't fix a daily budget that runs out by 11am.

The habit that keeps this from turning into guesswork is deciding, before you make a change, what you expect to see, by when, and what you'll do if you don't see it. That's the same standard we hold every automated change to at Dr. PPC — every proposed change carries the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger before it runs. Manual or automated, the discipline is identical: decide the undo before you need it.

The Mistakes That Waste the Budget

The most common mistake is optimizing on too little data — killing a keyword after four clicks and no sales, then watching a near-identical term convert at real volume the next week. We've done this ourselves: paused a term after a slow start, only to find it was building toward a spike that never got the chance to show up in the numbers.

The second is chasing ACOS toward zero. A campaign running 8% ACOS on $200 a month isn't healthier than one running 35% ACOS on $8,000 a month — the first is often just starving a keyword that could carry real volume. ACOS is a ratio; it tells you nothing about size until you also look at total contribution or TACOS.

The third is over-negating. Broad and phrase match exist to surface terms you didn't think of. Blocking anything that doesn't convert in week one, before the listing has had time to build relevance, closes off the exploration that broad match is for. Negatives belong on terms with real click volume and no sales — not on anything that's barely cleared double digits.

Where This Breaks Down at Scale

The loop above works cleanly for one keyword. Run 300 keywords across 15 campaigns and the same loop means reading thousands of rows a week, tracking which ones just cleared their click threshold, and remembering which bid changes are still inside their measurement window. Common questions

How often should I optimize an Amazon PPC campaign?

Check the search-term report and negatives weekly once a campaign has enough clicks to say anything. Revisit structure and campaign types monthly. Budget pacing is the exception — on accounts spending heavily, check daily so you're not running out by noon or sitting under-spent by evening.

What's a good ACOS target for Amazon PPC?

There isn't a universal number — it depends on your margin. Work out your breakeven ACOS (roughly your product margin before ad cost) and set the target under that, with room for the keywords still building organic rank. A 30% target that's fine for one product can be unprofitable for another with a thinner margin.

How many clicks before I can trust a keyword's data?

Around 10 clicks before you'll trust a zero-conversion result enough to add a negative, and roughly 20–30 clicks before a bid change based on ACOS means anything. Below that, differences in conversion rate are usually just sample noise, not signal.

Should I start with automatic or manual campaigns?

Automatic campaigns are the cheapest way to discover which search terms actually convert, because Amazon's algorithm is doing the matching for you. Once a term proves itself, move it into a manual exact or phrase campaign where you control the bid directly, and keep the automatic campaign running to keep discovering.

I raised a bid and nothing changed. What now?

Check impression share first — if you were already winning most available impressions, a higher bid has nowhere to go. Then check whether the budget capped the campaign before the bid change could take effect. If neither explains it, the constraint is probably relevance or the listing itself, not the bid.
