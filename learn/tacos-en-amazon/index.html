Two Different Searches Hiding Behind One Phrase

"Tacos en Amazon" and its English variants point to three unrelated things, and search results mix them constantly. If you're a seller, brand manager, or PPC person, you almost certainly want TACoS — Total Advertising Cost of Sale, a metric that measures ad spend against total revenue. If you searched in Spanish for shoes, "tacos" means heels, so "tacos nike en amazon" is a literal product search for Nike heeled shoes, not an advertising question at all. And if you just want dinner, Amazon sells taco holders, taco seasoning, and taco shells — real products, no metric involved.

This page is written for the first group, because that's where the actual work is. The rest of it covers what TACoS is, how to calculate it with real numbers, what a bad reading actually means, and where sellers most often get it wrong.

What TACoS Actually Measures

TACoS = Total Ad Spend ÷ Total Revenue (ad sales plus organic sales), shown as a percentage. It's one of the only common PPC metrics that includes organic sales in the denominator — which is the whole point. It tells you what advertising costs your entire business, not just the sales it can directly claim credit for.

ACoS, by contrast, only compares ad spend to ad-attributed sales. A campaign can carry a brutal 100% ACoS and still be doing its job if it's building organic rank that pays off later. TACoS is the number that tells you whether that bet actually worked.

A Worked Example With Real Numbers

Say a listing spends $4,000 a month on ads and does $50,000 in total revenue — $12,000 of that from ads, $38,000 organic. TACoS = $4,000 ÷ $50,000 = 8%. That same listing's ACoS is $4,000 ÷ $12,000 = 33%.

Both numbers are true at the same time. The 33% says ads are expensive relative to the sales they can directly claim. The 8% says advertising costs eight cents of every revenue dollar across the whole business. Sellers who only watch ACoS often panic at the 33% and cut spend — then watch organic sales fall too, because the ads were pulling more weight than the attribution window ever showed them.

When the Number Is Bad News

A rising TACoS after launch usually means one of three things: organic sales aren't picking up the slack the plan assumed they would, the category turned out more competitive than expected, or the math is simply wrong — most often because someone mixed a 30-day attribution window with a 7-day one, or compared months with very different promotional load.

If TACoS was trending down and then flattens, check whether the setting you think is doing the work — automatic bidding, a dynamic budget, an "always-on" campaign — is actually running, or has quietly been paused, exhausted its budget, or hit a portfolio cap for two weeks without anyone noticing.

If you cut spend and TACoS didn't improve, don't assume the cut failed before checking whether organic sales dropped too. That's the sign the spend was propping up rank rather than padding a vanity number, and the fix isn't "spend less" — it's "spend differently."

The Mistakes People Actually Make With TACoS

The most common one is treating TACoS as a single target to hit instead of a trend to read against a listing's own history. A 12% TACoS is normal three weeks after launch and a warning sign on a listing that's been live for two years with no promotional spend. A close second: comparing TACoS across products with very different price points inside one portfolio view — a $12 accessory and a $180 kitchen tool will never sit at the same TACoS, and shouldn't.

The third mistake — one that's easy to make managing a live account — is changing a bid strategy and a main listing image in the same week, then trying to read the TACoS movement as if it had one cause. Every proposed change should carry three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. Without that discipline, a TACoS chart tells you something moved, not what moved it or why.

Common questions

What does TACoS stand for on Amazon?

Total Advertising Cost of Sale. It's total ad spend divided by total revenue, where total revenue includes both ad-attributed sales and organic sales — not just the sales ads can directly claim credit for.

Is TACoS the same as ACoS?

No. ACoS divides ad spend by ad-attributed sales only. TACoS divides ad spend by total revenue, ad plus organic. A listing can have a high ACoS and a low TACoS at the same time, and that's usually a good sign, not a bad one.

What's a good TACoS for a new product launch?

There's no single number that applies across categories or margins. The useful pattern is direction: TACoS is expected to run high right after launch and should trend down as organic rank builds. A number that stays flat or climbs for months, with no promotional activity to explain it, is the thing worth investigating.

Does Amazon calculate TACoS for me in Seller Central or the Ads console?

No. Amazon doesn't surface TACoS as a built-in metric in either system. Ad spend lives in Amazon Ads reporting and total revenue lives in Seller Central; sellers pull both and calculate the ratio themselves, or use a tool that already lines the two up.

Why did I get shoes or taco holders when I searched "tacos en amazon"?

Because the phrase is genuinely ambiguous. In Spanish, "tacos" means heels, so a query like "tacos nike en amazon" is a literal search for Nike heeled shoes. Amazon's own search also returns actual taco-related products — seasoning, holders, shells — for anyone shopping rather than asking about advertising.
