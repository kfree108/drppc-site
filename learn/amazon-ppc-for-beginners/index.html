What Amazon PPC Is, With Real Numbers

Amazon PPC is the ad system built into Seller Central and Vendor Central. You bid on a keyword. If you win the auction, your product shows as a sponsored listing in search results or on a competitor's product page. You pay only when someone clicks — not for the impression, and win or lose, you never pay more than one cent above the next highest bid.

The metric that decides whether any of this is worth it is ACoS: ad spend divided by ad revenue. Here's a worked example most guides skip. Say you bid $0.75 a click on a campaign, it runs one week, and gets 120 clicks. That's $90 spent. Of those 120 clicks, 3 people buy, at $25 each — $75 in ad revenue. ACoS is $90 ÷ $75 = 120%.

That number alone doesn't tell you if you're losing money — you need your margin. If that $25 product leaves $9 of profit after cost of goods, FBA fees, and the referral fee, your break-even ACoS is $9 ÷ $25 = 36%. At 120% ACoS, you spent $90 to earn back $27 in actual margin (3 units × $9). That week cost you $63, not $90 — but it still cost you. This is the calculation to run before you decide a campaign is working, not impressions or clicks alone.

The Ad Types You'll Actually Use First

There are more ad types than a beginner needs on day one. Three matter early:

  • Sponsored Products — shows in search results and on other product pages. No brand registry required. This is where almost every beginner starts, and where the worked example above applies directly.
  • Sponsored Brands — requires Brand Registry. Shows your logo and multiple products near the top of search results. Better for awareness once you have more than one SKU worth cross-promoting.
  • Sponsored Display — targets shoppers by audience or by competing product, and can follow them off Amazon. Useful for remarketing once you have traffic history, not a starting point.

Inside Sponsored Products you'll choose automatic or manual targeting. Automatic hands keyword selection to Amazon's algorithm and is the right choice for a brand-new listing with no data. Manual gives you control over bids per keyword, but only pays off once you know which keywords convert — which is the entire point of the next section.

Your First 30 Days: Automatic, Then Manual

The sequence that works is the same one every experienced PPC manager runs, and it's simpler than most tutorials make it sound. Launch automatic, read what it tells you, then take control.

The Mistake That Quietly Kills Beginner Accounts

It isn't bad targeting. It's stopping the loop above after month one or two — leaving an automatic campaign running on autopilot for a year with the same bids it launched with, because nobody went back to read the search term report a second time.

Across the more than $500 million in Amazon ad spend Full Circle has managed across 100+ brands, this is the single most common failure pattern we see when we take over an account: not one catastrophic error, just a harvest loop that ran once and never ran again. We've seen it happen inside accounts we manage too, before it got caught — this isn't a mistake unique to beginners.

The discipline that fixes it doesn't require software. It requires writing down, before you change a bid or add a keyword, three things: what evidence made you do it, how you'll measure whether it worked, and at what point you undo it if it didn't. That's the same structure we hold every proposed change to — every proposed change carries three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. You can run that discipline manually with a spreadsheet. Most beginners just never write it down, so nothing gets reverted and nothing gets repeated.

When Your Numbers Look Bad

Bad numbers are normal in the first few weeks. What matters is knowing which fix to try first.

  • ACoS spikes suddenly: check whether a competitor entered the same keyword, your price changed, or your organic rank dropped — PPC often absorbs the blame for problems that started elsewhere on the listing.
  • Impressions but no clicks: your bid is probably too low for the placement you're chasing, or your main image and price aren't competitive against what's beating you in that search result.
  • Clicks but no sales: check the actual search term report before touching the bid. A broad match keyword is often pulling in traffic that was never going to convert — that's a targeting problem, not a bid problem.
  • Attribution looks off: Amazon's sales reporting lags behind the click, so don't judge a change on same-day numbers. Give any single change at least a week before you decide it failed.

If a fix doesn't move the number in a week, revert it before you try a second fix. Stacking changes on top of an unproven change is how beginner accounts turn into ones nobody can explain anymore.

Common questions

How much budget does a beginner need to start Amazon PPC?

There's no universal minimum — it depends on your CPC range and how many keywords you're testing. Start small enough that a bad week doesn't hurt, but large enough to generate the 100+ clicks you need before a search term report tells you anything reliable. A budget too small just delays the data you need to make the first real decision.

What's a good ACoS for a beginner?

There's no fixed good number — it's whatever's below your break-even ACoS, which is your product margin as a percentage of price. Calculate that first. A 40% ACoS can be profitable on one product and a loss on another, depending on what's left after cost of goods and Amazon's fees.

Should I run automatic or manual campaigns first?

Automatic first, always, for a brand-new listing. It has no keyword history to draw on, so let Amazon's algorithm find converting terms for one to two weeks, then move the winners into manual campaigns where you control the bid directly.

Why do my Amazon PPC sales numbers keep changing after I check them?

Attribution isn't instant — a click today can show as a sale several days later, and Amazon's reports update retroactively. Don't judge a campaign change on same-day or next-day numbers. Give it a full week minimum before deciding it worked or didn't.

What's the single biggest mistake beginners make in Amazon PPC?

Running the harvest loop once at launch and never running it again. An automatic campaign left untouched for months keeps paying the same bids on the same mix of good and bad search terms indefinitely — the fix isn't more spend, it's going back to the report.
