What's actually behind this search

There are two unrelated things sharing the word "sponsored," and search engines have mashed them together. The first is a real, public announcement: in January 2024, Byrna Technologies signed Glenn Beck and his company, Blaze Media, for a 52-week radio and TV campaign across The Glenn Beck Program and Blaze TV. That's a media sponsorship — Byrna bought air time and a paid endorsement, the same way any brand buys a spot on a show with an audience it wants.

The second thing, older and separate, is a 2009 boycott campaign where an advocacy group pressured companies to stop buying ad space during Beck's Fox News program. That's also a media-advertising story, not an Amazon one. Neither of these has anything to do with Amazon's own ad product called "Sponsored Products." If you landed here because you're managing an Amazon account and saw this phrase somewhere, the two are worth untangling before you make any decision based on either.

What Amazon actually calls Sponsored Products

Sponsored Products is Amazon's cost-per-click ad format that puts a single product listing into search results and on competing product pages. You pick keywords or let Amazon's automatic targeting choose them, you set a bid, and you pay only when someone clicks — win the auction for that search, get the click, pay the price the auction set. There's no host, no script, no endorsement; it's an auction running continuously against every other seller bidding on the same terms.

That's the structural difference that matters. A media sponsorship like Byrna's is a negotiated deal for airtime and a spoken endorsement. Sponsored Products is a self-service or agency-managed auction that any seller with a product listing can enter, with spend that moves up and down daily depending on competition and demand.

The worked example: how a media push shows up inside an Amazon ads account

Byrna's own numbers are useful here because they show the mechanism, even though they're not an Amazon PPC report. In the press release, Byrna said its new advertising approach — including the early Beck placements — boosted web sessions nearly 70% and raised conversion rate 10% in the first seven weeks of the fiscal year, with daily average sessions rising from 32,502 in Q4 2023 to 35,352 in December 2023.

That's traffic to Byrna's own site, not Amazon. But if a brand sells on Amazon and runs a similar off-platform push, the effect shows up inside the ad account in a predictable way: branded search volume rises, competitors sometimes start bidding on the brand's own name to catch the overflow, and branded cost-per-click can climb even though nothing in the Amazon campaign itself changed. The account looks like it got more expensive. What actually happened is demand went up somewhere else first.

The mistake we've watched teams make — and made ourselves

The common error is crediting the wrong channel. A brand runs a radio spot, sees a branded-keyword sales bump on Amazon three days later, and concludes the PPC campaign is suddenly performing better — so they raise bids further and shift budget toward that keyword. The bump wasn't the campaign working harder. It was the radio spot sending people to search the brand name, and the PPC campaign just happened to be the thing standing there to catch them.

The reverse mistake is just as common: branded ACOS spikes because a competitor started conquesting the brand name at the same time the media placement ran, someone reads the higher ACOS as inefficiency, and pauses the branded campaign entirely — which hands that traffic straight to the competitor's ad instead.

  • Before touching a bid, check the search term report for new competitor activity on your own brand terms.
  • Before crediting a lift to PPC, check whether the timing lines up with anything running off-Amazon.
  • Before pausing anything, confirm the number is actually wrong and not just unfamiliar.

What to do when the number looks wrong

If branded ACOS jumps right after an outside media placement, don't treat it as a verdict on the campaign. Isolate the exact-match brand defense line from the rest of the account, look at whether impression share on your own brand term dropped, and hold off on a bid change for a few days of data — auctions on brand terms settle once competitors decide whether the conquesting is worth it.

Any change worth making on the back of that data should carry three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. That's not a slogan, it's the difference between a fix and a guess — if you raise a bid because of a media-driven spike, decide in advance what result would tell you to reverse it, and by when.

Which one you should actually pick

Media sponsorships suit brands with the budget for a sustained, story-driven campaign and the patience for slower, harder-to-isolate attribution. Amazon Sponsored Products suits any seller who wants to capture demand — including demand a media deal just created — at the moment someone searches. Most brands running one need the other; they're not substitutes.

Common questions

Does Amazon have a 'Glenn Beck' sponsored product?

No. Amazon's Sponsored Products ad format has no connection to Glenn Beck. What people are actually finding is Byrna Technologies' 2024 media partnership with Beck and Blaze Media — a radio and TV advertising deal, not an Amazon ad placement.

Is the Color of Change boycott related to Amazon advertising?

No. That campaign, from 2009, pressured TV and radio advertisers to stop buying ad time on Glenn Beck's Fox News program. It predates Amazon's Sponsored Products program by years and involves a completely different set of advertisers and a different medium.

Can a celebrity endorsement change what I pay for Amazon Sponsored Products?

Indirectly, yes. If the endorsement drives people to search your brand name, competitors may start bidding on that name too, which can raise your branded cost-per-click even though your own campaign settings haven't changed. Check the search term report before assuming your account got less efficient.

How do I tell if a sales spike came from PPC or an outside media push?

Compare the timing against your media schedule, check whether the lift shows up in both organic and sponsored branded traffic (a media push tends to lift both), and resist crediting the ad campaign for a result it didn't cause.

Should I still run Sponsored Products if I already have a media endorsement deal?

Generally yes. The media placement creates demand off Amazon; Sponsored Products is often what catches the shopper once they search for you on Amazon. If you don't hold your own brand and category terms, a competitor's ad can catch that traffic instead.
