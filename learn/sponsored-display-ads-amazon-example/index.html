What a Sponsored Display ad actually looks like

Open a competitor's product page and look just below the description, past the "customers also viewed" strip. That gray-bordered box with someone else's product image and a "Sponsored" label is a Sponsored Display ad. It's the same ad type whether it's sitting on a rival's listing, following you onto a recipe site three days after you looked at a blender, or showing up on a Fire TV home screen.

Amazon folded the old "Sponsored Display" name into a broader "display ads" hub inside its console, alongside DSP. If you already have campaigns running, nothing changed — they still live under Campaign Manager and bid the same way. New campaigns get built through the same "Display" option, just inside the unified interface.

  • On a competitor's listing — a small box below the description, showing your product image, price, and a "Shop now" button.
  • On your own product page — a carousel of related items competing for the same shopper's attention.
  • Off Amazon — a banner on a news site, app, or recipe blog, showing the exact product a shopper viewed and didn't buy.
  • On Amazon devices — Fire TV home screens, Echo Show, and, for larger advertisers, Twitch.

The three targeting types, and what each one looks like

Sponsored Display doesn't target keywords. It targets a shopper's behavior or a specific product page, and which option you pick changes both where the ad shows and what it's for.

  • Product targeting — you pick ASINs or categories, often a competitor's, and your ad appears on those listings. Open to sellers in Brand Registry and to vendors.
  • Views remarketing — you target people who looked at your (or a similar) product page in the last 30 days without buying. The ad follows them around the web and back onto Amazon. Also open to sellers and vendors.
  • Audience / interest targeting — Amazon shows your ad to shoppers who've signaled interest in a related category, whether or not they've ever seen your listing. This one is vendor-only.

Amazon has published that a majority of sales from its Sponsored Display audience targeting come from shoppers new to the brand — which tracks, since interest targeting is built for people who've never seen the product, not people finishing a purchase they already started.

A worked example: how the budget turns into an order

Here's the mechanic, using round numbers to show how the pieces connect. Swap in your own account's figures once you see the shape of it.

You set a views-remarketing group with a $0.65 max CPC and a $25 daily budget. The budget alone caps you at roughly 38 clicks a day if every one landed at your max bid — but display placements rarely spend that cleanly, because most of the budget buys impressions nobody clicks. Say you actually get 20 clicks. If 1 in 10 converts, that's 2 orders a day. At a $40 average order value, that's $80 in sales against roughly $13 spent — an ACOS around 16%, before you've touched product targeting or audience at all.

The point isn't the specific percentages — yours will differ by category and price point. It's that budget caps volume, bid caps placement quality, and conversion rate is the only lever display ads share with an actual sale. Raise the bid without raising the budget and you buy better placement on the same small pool of clicks, not more of them.

The mistake that quietly wastes the budget

The most common one: leaving Amazon's auto-generated creative on a product-targeting campaign that's going to sit on a competitor's page. That creative pulls your existing listing image and title, which is fine on your own product page — but on a rival's listing it blends into a row of other ads doing the same thing. If the competitor's price is lower, that ad converts close to zero, and nobody notices for weeks because the campaign is technically "running."

A second one, and one we've had to fix on live accounts: leaving a views-remarketing audience without a purchases exclusion. The shopper already bought — from you or from someone else — and the ad keeps following them anyway, spending against a sale that's already closed. The exclusion sits in the same audience panel as the targeting settings. It's just not switched on by default.

When the example doesn't match what you're seeing

If your Sponsored Display ad isn't showing anywhere you can find it, check three things before assuming the campaign is broken:

  • Eligibility — interest/audience targeting only runs for vendors. If you're a seller and picked "Audiences," you're actually in views remarketing or Amazon audiences, not the vendor-only interest pool.
  • The 30-day window — views remarketing only reaches people who looked in the last 30 days. A product with a long consideration cycle can run the audience dry well before the budget runs out.
  • Budget versus reach — a $5–$10 daily budget on product targeting against a high-traffic competitor ASIN can lose the auction before it spends meaningfully. Near-zero impressions usually means the bid or budget is too low for that placement, not that the ad is malformed.

If the ad is showing and still not converting, the creative is almost always the first thing to change — not the bid.

Which one you should actually pick

Product targeting suits anyone who wants to intercept traffic on a specific competitor's listing. Views remarketing suits categories with a real consideration window — it's wasted on impulse buys under $15. Audience/interest targeting is vendor-only and built for awareness, not immediate sales. Amazon's own pages explain the definitions well; they stop before the budget math and the troubleshooting, which is where most accounts actually lose money.

Common questions

What's the difference between a Sponsored Display example and a Sponsored Brands example?

Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Products run against keyword searches at the top of Amazon's results page. Sponsored Display doesn't target keywords at all — it targets a shopper's behavior or product-page views, and it can show off Amazon entirely, which is why you'll see it on a competitor's listing or on a third-party site.

Can I use interest/audience targeting as a third-party seller?

No. Interest and audience targeting is vendor-only. Sellers enrolled in Brand Registry get product targeting and views remarketing, which cover retargeting and competitor interception but not the cold, category-level audience reach vendors get.

Do Sponsored Display ads need custom creative, or does Amazon generate it?

Amazon will auto-generate creative from your listing image and title if you don't supply your own. That's fine on your own product page, but on a competitor's page it often blends in and underperforms — a custom logo and headline generally does better there.

Why did Amazon rename Sponsored Display?

Amazon folded it into a unified "display ads" hub alongside its DSP product, to put both under one console. Existing campaigns kept running unchanged under Campaign Manager; new campaigns get built through the same interface, just relabeled.

Is there a minimum budget for Sponsored Display?

No fixed minimum — you set a daily budget and a bid, similar to Sponsored Products. In practice, a budget that's too low relative to your bid on a competitive ASIN will lose most auctions before it spends meaningfully.
