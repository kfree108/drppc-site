What "Next Page of Related Sponsored Products" Actually Means

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1335 words · Written against what currently ranked for “next page of related sponsored products”
The short answer

There's no single Amazon feature by this name. It covers two real things: sponsored ads that continue onto search-result page 2 and beyond (Rest of Search), and the "Sponsored products related to this item" carousel on product pages, which loads more ads when you click its next arrow.

What this phrase is actually asking about

Amazon doesn't have a feature with this exact name. The phrase is doing double duty for two separate, real things sellers run into: sponsored ads that keep showing up after you page past the first screen of search results, and the "Sponsored products related to this item" carousel that sits lower on a product detail page.

The first is a placement question: does Amazon still show ads on search page 2, 3, and beyond, or does sponsored inventory stop after page 1? The second is a widget question: that carousel only shows a few tiles at a time, and clicking its arrow loads more — sellers want to know how many ads live in there and whether that "next page" click is something they can influence.

They get lumped together because both involve a shopper clicking to see more sponsored listings. Answering them separately is the only way to actually act on the answer.

Search page 2 and beyond: this is Rest of Search

Amazon Ads groups Sponsored Products inventory into three placements: Top of Search (above the first row of organic results, page 1 only), Rest of Search (interspersed through organic listings on page 1 and every page after it), and Product Pages. Rest of Search is what "next page" really refers to — your ad can keep appearing as a shopper pages deeper into results, at whatever density the auction produces for that query on that page.

Amazon doesn't publish a fixed count of how many sponsored slots sit on page 2 versus page 1 — it varies by query, category, and how many advertisers are bidding at that moment. What you can see is the placement report inside campaign reporting, which breaks impressions, clicks, and spend out by Top of Search, Rest of Search, and Product Pages. That's the real answer to "count next page of related sponsored products." There's no export that says page 2, position 4. There's a placement-level report that tells you, in aggregate, how much of your budget and how many of your clicks came from Rest of Search versus Top of Search.

The carousel on the product page

Separately, "Sponsored products related to this item" is a real widget. It sits below the buy box or below the reviews on a detail page and shows other sellers' ads targeted to that ASIN through product targeting. It displays a handful of tiles — usually visible without scrolling — and a next arrow reveals more. Amazon doesn't publish a fixed size for that pool, and it isn't the same for every shopper: it's re-run through the ad auction, so the tiles visible today can differ from what a different shopper on a different device sees an hour later.

If you're trying to land in that carousel, the lever isn't a page count — it's product targeting. You choose the ASINs or categories you want your ad to appear against, bid competitively for that targeting, and make sure the product is in stock and winning the Buy Box, because Amazon won't show an ad for a listing that isn't eligible to sell.

When the number looks wrong

Two things send sellers looking for a "count" in the first place: a rank tracker or third-party tool reports a figure that doesn't match what they see when they check manually, or Rest of Search spend looks high relative to Top of Search and they want to know if they're paying for placements they can't verify. Both have the same root cause — the ad auction is real-time and personalized, so any snapshot a tool takes is already out of date by the time you read it.

If Rest of Search spend is climbing and conversion is falling, don't start by hunting for a placement count. Pull the placement report for that campaign and compare cost-per-click and conversion rate across Top of Search, Rest of Search, and Product Pages for that specific product. If Rest of Search is buying clicks at a similar CPC to Top of Search but converting worse, lower the bid multiplier on Rest of Search rather than pulling the whole campaign.

The mistake, including one we've made

The mistake we've made ourselves: treating Rest of Search as automatically cheap inventory because CPCs there tend to run lower, and pushing bids up across the board on that assumption. On some catalogs that's true — the discount holds and margin improves. On others, a shopper three pages deep into search results is further from a purchase decision than one on page one, and the conversion drop cancels out the cheaper click.

Across the accounts we manage — more than $500M in tracked Amazon ad spend across 100+ brands, catalogs as different as HexClad's cookware and Beardbrand's grooming line — placement behavior is one of the most product-specific variables there is. A rule that holds for one catalog can invert for the next one over. The only reliable check is that product's own placement report, not a rule of thumb carried over from a different account.

Which one you should actually pick

Sellers running a handful of campaigns can read their own placement report and adjust bid multipliers by hand — it takes minutes per product per week. Sellers with dozens of SKUs, where the right placement bid differs product to product, are the ones for whom checking this consistently becomes the actual job, whether they automate it themselves or have something else do it.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Can I guarantee my product shows in the related sponsored products carousel?

No. It's decided by the ad auction each time a page loads, based on your product targeting, bid, and whether the product is in stock and winning the Buy Box. You can improve your odds by targeting relevant ASINs and bidding competitively, but there's no setting that locks in a slot.

Does Amazon show sponsored ads on every page of search results, or just page one?

Rest of Search placements continue past page one — there's no rule that sponsored inventory stops after the first screen. Top of Search is the exception: it only appears above the very first row of results on page one.

Why does a rank tracker show a different count than what I see when I check manually?

The auction is real-time and personalized to the shopper, device, and moment. A third-party tool's snapshot is one instance of that auction, not a fixed inventory count, so two checks minutes apart can legitimately differ.

Is Rest of Search always cheaper than Top of Search?

CPCs there tend to run lower, but lower CPC doesn't automatically mean better return. Check the conversion rate for that specific product before assuming the cheaper click is the better one — on some catalogs it isn't.

Where do I actually see the breakdown between these placements?

In your Sponsored Products campaign reporting, filtered or broken out by placement. It shows impressions, clicks, and spend for Top of Search, Rest of Search, and Product Pages separately, which is the closest thing to the "count" most sellers are actually after.
