What a product targeting campaign actually is

A product targeting campaign — more precisely, a campaign using the product targeting option — puts your ad in front of shoppers looking at a specific ASIN or category page, instead of matching a search term. It's a manual targeting choice inside Sponsored Products or Sponsored Display, sitting alongside keyword targeting and automatic targeting, not a separate campaign type with its own line item on the campaign creation screen.

The distinction that trips people up: automatic targeting also shows your ad on other products' pages, using its close match, loose match, substitutes, and complements logic. Manual product targeting does the same kind of thing but with you, not Amazon, choosing which ASINs or categories qualify. You get a list, you pick, you bid per target.

Negative targeting is the third piece — excluding specific ASINs, brands, or categories you don't want your ad to appear against, on either automatic or manual campaigns.

Sponsored Products vs Sponsored Display: same idea, different reach

Both ad types offer product targeting, and they put you in different places.

  • Sponsored Products product targeting shows your ad in search results and on product detail pages, usually lower on the page or inside a carousel, and always sends the click to your product detail page.
  • Sponsored Display product targeting can follow the shopper further — onto detail pages, alongside reviews, in higher-visibility placements — and lets you use a custom headline and logo, which Sponsored Products does not.

If you're new to this and only have budget for one, start with Sponsored Products product targeting on a short list of ASINs. Sponsored Display's reach is wider, which is an advantage once you know which targets convert and a disadvantage while you're still finding out.

How to build one, with real numbers

Say you sell a $34 stainless coffee maker and want to target five competitor ASINs that show up above you in search. You build a Sponsored Products campaign, choose manual targeting, then product targeting, and enter those five ASINs directly rather than letting Amazon suggest them.

Each ASIN gets its own bid. If the category's suggested bid range sits around $0.60–$1.10, you might open at $0.75 on the two closest competitors and $0.55 on three more tangential ones — coffee makers at a similar price point rather than the exact same feature set. That spread matters more than the total budget: a flat bid across five very different ASINs almost always overpays for the weak targets and underpays for the strong ones.

After the first real week of data — not day three — you'll have a click count and a conversion count per ASIN, not per campaign. That's the number to act on. Clicks with zero orders after a reasonable sample isn't a targeting problem yet; it might just need more volume before you can tell anything. Clicks, orders, and an ACOS you can live with is the one you raise the bid on.

The mistakes that waste spend on product targeting

The most common one: targeting an entire category instead of specific ASINs. We've done this ourselves — set a Sponsored Display campaign to target "coffee makers" broadly instead of the dozen ASINs that were the actual competitive set, and watched it spend evenly across everything from budget machines to espresso rigs that share nothing with the product but a category node. Impressions were fine. Conversions weren't, because most of that traffic had no reason to want what we were selling.

Second: bidding the same amount you'd bid on a keyword campaign. Product targeting placements convert differently — someone looking at a competitor's detail page is closer to a purchase decision than someone typing a broad search term, but they're also more loyal to what they're already looking at. There's no universal rule for which way that pushes your bid; check your own conversion rate on the placement before assuming it should match your keyword bids.

Third: forgetting to check whether your own ASINs are getting caught in someone else's targeting, or targeting your own catalog without a reason. Defensive targeting — putting your ad on your own product's page, or on a lower-tier SKU in your own line to move shoppers up — is a legitimate use of the format, but only if you set it up on purpose, not because a category target swept it in by accident.

What to do when the campaign isn't working

Before touching a bid, separate "no data yet" from "bad target." A handful of clicks with no orders is not evidence of anything at low volume. Wait for a sample size that matches how often that ASIN actually gets clicked on.

Once you have a real sample, check the target before the bid. A target with clicks and no sales at a reasonable sample is usually the wrong ASIN, not the wrong bid — no amount of bid increase fixes a category mismatch. A target with sales but an ACOS you don't like is a bid problem, and that's where a manual adjustment or dynamic bidding earns its keep.

This is the same discipline we run on every account at Full Circle, across more than $500M in managed Amazon ad spend: no change goes live without the evidence behind it, a measurement plan for what "working" looks like, and a rollback trigger for when it doesn't. Product targeting decisions made without that structure usually just accumulate — nobody prunes the ASIN list, and six months later half the targets are dead weight nobody remembers adding.

Which one you should actually pick

Automatic targeting suits a brand-new campaign or a fast way to find manual targets. Manual product targeting suits anyone who already knows which ASINs or categories matter and wants to control the bid on each one directly. It rewards regular pruning; it punishes anyone who sets it and doesn't come back.

Common questions

Is product targeting the same as automatic targeting?

No. Automatic targeting lets Amazon choose which ASINs and categories your ad matches, using close match, loose match, substitutes, and complements. Manual product targeting is you picking the specific ASINs or categories yourself. Many advertisers run automatic first to see which ASINs Amazon surfaces, then build a manual product targeting campaign around the ones that actually convert.

Can I target my own ASINs?

Yes — it's a legitimate use of the format, usually to keep a shopper's attention on your own catalog rather than a competitor's, or to move traffic between SKUs in your own line. Do it deliberately, not as a side effect of a category target that's too broad.

What bid should I start with on a product target?

There's no fixed number that holds across categories. Amazon's suggested bid range in the console reflects current competition for that specific ASIN or category, and it moves. Start inside that range, weight it by how close the target actually is to your product, and adjust off your own click and conversion data rather than a number from a blog post.

Does Sponsored Brands support product targeting?

Sponsored Products and Sponsored Display are where manual ASIN and category targeting is built the way this guide describes. Sponsored Brands' targeting options are more limited and change more often than the other two, so check the current options in the campaign creation screen before assuming it works the same way.

How long do I wait before judging a target?

Long enough to get a real click sample on that specific ASIN, not the campaign as a whole — a week is a reasonable floor for most categories, longer for lower-traffic ones. Judging a target on day three punishes it for a sample that was never big enough to say anything.
