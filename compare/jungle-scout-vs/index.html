Three products, one brand name, and a lot of confused comparisons

The reason Jungle Scout comparisons feel slippery is that the name covers three different things sold to three different buyers.

  • Catalyst — self-serve software aimed at sellers below roughly a million dollars a year in revenue. Product research, demand estimates, keyword work, the tools that made the brand.
  • Cobalt — the brand and enterprise platform above that line. Category and market-share intelligence, competitor and price tracking, share of voice. Sold through a demo.
  • The advertising layer — technology Jungle Scout acquired when it bought an Amazon adtech business several years ago, run for a period under its original name and now merged into Cobalt.

A published comparison that says Jungle Scout is weak on advertising is usually describing Catalyst. One that says it has serious media capability is describing Cobalt. Both can be published in the same month and neither is lying.

So the first question is not which competitor is better. It is which of the three is in the proposal in front of you. If a salesperson has shown you category share dashboards, you are being sold Cobalt and your competitive set is enterprise commerce platforms. If you signed up online with a card, you have Catalyst and your competitive set is research tools. These shortlists have almost no overlap.

You cannot build this comparison on price, and here is why

Ordinarily the first column in a comparison table is cost. In this case that column is unreliable in both halves of the product line, so build the comparison on capability and contract terms instead and verify the money separately.

Cobalt is quoted after a demo. Nothing is published, which is a normal enterprise practice and simply means the figure is yours to negotiate rather than yours to look up.

Catalyst is more unusual. Its plan prices are drawn by JavaScript after the page loads and are varied between visitors, so two people reading the same URL on the same afternoon can see different sets. That is standard conversion testing and there is nothing improper in it — but it does mean any article quoting Catalyst tier prices, including the ones ranking above this one, is quoting a snapshot of one visitor's session. We are not going to print figures we cannot guarantee will match your screen.

The practical method takes about five minutes: open the pricing page yourself, put the plan you want in the cart, and read the checkout total including any annual discount and tax. That number is real. Everything else is a screenshot of somebody else's test group.

Then compare it against rivals that do publish. Helium 10 lists Diamond at $359 a month, or $279 billed annually, and states that Diamond customers incur a 2% management fee on PPC spend managed through Helium 10 Ads. That is a complete, checkable cost, and it is the cleanest anchor available in this category.

If you are comparing Catalyst: research against research

Here the honest competitor set is other research tools, and Jungle Scout's advantage is not data volume — it is that the workflow finishes.

A first-time seller can open Catalyst, filter a category, look at demand and competition estimates, and reach a decision without learning a vocabulary first. That sounds like a small thing until you watch the alternative: a deeper suite where the same person opens four tools, gets four partial answers and closes the tab. Jungle Scout's design has always favoured getting to an answer over exposing every variable, and for the buyer it is aimed at, that is the correct trade.

Where a rival takes it: a broader suite wins if you also need listing optimisation, refund claims, alerts and ad automation from the same subscription, because bundles beat point tools once you genuinely use four or more modules. A cheaper narrow tool wins if discovery is the only job and you have already learned the category.

The test that settles it is unglamorous. Look at when you last opened your research tool. If it was more than a month ago, you are not choosing between research products — you are paying a research subscription while your actual problem is operational, and the comparison belongs in the next two sections.

If you are comparing Cobalt: intelligence against media operations

At this altitude the competitive set changes completely, and it is worth being clear about what Cobalt's genuine edge is, because it is not advertising.

Jungle Scout's underlying asset has always been category-level market data. Cobalt turns that into the answer to a board-level question: what share of our category do we hold, how has it moved, who took it, and at what price points. Very few platforms answer that as directly, and the advertising layer sitting on top means you can act on the finding without exporting to another tool.

The rivals, and what each genuinely wins on:

  • Pacvue — retail media breadth. It lists 100+ retailers across 30+ countries and positions itself as a commerce media operating system. If your plan spans Amazon, Walmart, Instacart and Target, that is not a close contest.
  • DataHawk — the data layer. Snowflake, Power BI, Looker Studio and Sheets, with an explicit no-lock-in position on your own operational data. The right answer if you have an analyst who would rather query than click. It quotes custom pricing on annual plans.
  • Intentwise — measurement depth, particularly Amazon Marketing Cloud, with a product line now organised around pipelines, diagnostics and optimisation.

Frame the decision as which single question you are buying an answer to. Market share, retail media breadth, or a queryable data layer. Cobalt wins the first outright and competes on the others rather than dominating them.

The comparison that never appears in the roundups

Every option above, at both altitudes, shares one property: it is something you operate. It tells you things. What it does with the telling is up to you and the hours you have.

That is where the money is lost, and it is invisible in a feature comparison. In Walkize, an account we are cleared to name publicly, $89,885 of ad spend — 33.6% of everything spent between December 2025 and August 2026 — went to search terms that produced no orders at all. That was 89,045 individual terms, none of them big enough to look like a problem on their own, against a top 1% of terms that drove 75.5% of all sales.

No research platform would have flagged that, because it was not a research question. No dashboard flagged it either, because on the summary view the account looked reasonable at 2.25x and 44.4% ACOS. It took somebody reading the whole search-term report against what each product actually costs to make.

That is the comparison worth running once you have shortlisted software: this platform, versus having the decision made and defended by someone accountable for it.

Which one you should actually pick

If the question is what to sell next, Jungle Scout's self-serve product is one of the easiest ways to reach an answer and worth its price. If the question is category share reported upward, Cobalt is a serious platform with a genuine data advantage. If the question is who actually decides what changes in the ad account this week and stands behind it, that is not what either product is for, and Dr. PPC is.

What to do with this

Neither of these decides your ACoS on its own — how much of the work gets done each week does. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders. On the account above it was 33.6%. Pick the option that leaves someone actually working that list, whether that is you or us.

Common questions

Is Jungle Scout better than Helium 10?

For a first-time seller who wants to reach a decision quickly, Jungle Scout's workflow is easier to finish. For someone who will use four or more modules and wants depth in keywords, listings and ad automation, the broader suite tends to win on value. Neither runs your ad account for you.

Does Jungle Scout manage Amazon ads?

Advertising capability exists inside Cobalt, from technology Jungle Scout acquired and merged in, and it is aimed at brands rather than self-serve sellers. The self-serve product is a research tool. If a comparison tells you Jungle Scout has no advertising features, it is describing the self-serve side only.

Why do published Jungle Scout prices keep changing?

The self-serve pricing renders in JavaScript after the page loads and is tested across visitors, so different readers see different sets on the same day, and articles quoting tiers are quoting one session. The enterprise side is quoted after a demo. Price it from your own checkout screen instead.

Which Jungle Scout alternative is best for advertising specifically?

It depends on the failing job. Retailer breadth points to Pacvue, goal-based automation to Perpetua, human help attached to a platform to BidX, measurement depth to Intentwise. If the failing job is that nobody has the hours to decide what should change, none of those fill it.

Can I use a research tool and a managed service together?

Yes, and it is a sensible combination — they answer different questions. Keep the research subscription for what to sell next, and let the management layer own what happens in the ad account. The only thing to avoid is two systems adjusting the same bids, which produces changes nobody can attribute.
