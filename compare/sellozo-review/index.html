Sellozo review: a good visual campaign builder on a single flat plan

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1807 words · Written against what currently ranked for “sellozo review”
The short answer

Sellozo is Amazon PPC automation, reported as acquired by the ecommerce accelerator Netrush in October 2021. It now sells one flat-rate plan covering a chosen number of marketplaces, with a 30-day free trial and no percentage of ad spend. Campaign Studio, its visual campaign builder, is the most-praised part.

Who owns it, and why that matters to you

Netrush, a Portland-based ecommerce accelerator, announced its acquisition of Sellozo on 28 October 2021. That comes from the acquisition announcement and the trade coverage around it rather than from Sellozo's own site, which does not foreground it — worth knowing so you can ask rather than assume. The product has continued to run under its own name and its own pricing page since.

What it changes is who you are buying from. Sellozo sits inside a group that also sells services to brands. That can be a strength — service teams using their own tooling every day tend to keep it grounded — and it can be a distraction, if the roadmap starts serving the parent's accounts before it serves yours. Ask directly on the call: who is the product team now, how many people, and what shipped in the last two quarters.

Campaign Studio is the reason people like it

Strip away the marketing and the distinctive thing Sellozo built is a visual campaign builder. You lay campaigns out on a canvas, connect them, and see the structure of your account as a shape rather than as a spreadsheet with four hundred rows. Users describe it as one of the more intuitive interfaces in a category not famous for them.

That matters more than it sounds. Most Amazon accounts are a mess of structure inherited from whoever ran them two agencies ago, and the single hardest thing to hold in your head is how campaigns relate to each other — which one is harvesting, which one is defending, which one has been quietly duplicating another for a year. A tool that draws that picture earns its place.

Around it sit the expected pieces: automated bid adjustments, keyword and negative management, dayparting, and reporting. Reviewers on service-focused directories are generally warm, citing sales up and ACOS down, and support gets consistently decent marks.

The pricing, which is now a single line

Sellozo has simplified to one plan, and the page states it plainly under the heading Flat Rate Pricing, with the promise of no percentage of ad spend and no hidden fees.

  • Sellozo Platform — $250 a month for one marketplace, with a slider that runs from one to twenty marketplaces as you add them.
  • A 30-day free trial, and month-to-month billing rather than an annual term.
  • Unlimited users on the account, which is not universal in this category and is quietly useful once more than one person touches the ads.

Two things follow. First, the pricing trail that used to make Sellozo hard to evaluate — a five-step ladder of consultation tiers, dated to 2023, still quoted across roundups — has been replaced by something you can read in ten seconds. If you are working from an old comparison table, throw it out. Second, this is a genuinely low-commitment evaluation: a month free, then a month at a time, on a published number.

The marketplace slider is the part to model rather than skim. Price it against the marketplaces you will actually run in twelve months, and ask what counts as a marketplace on your particular account before you compare the total with anyone else's.

The block on their pricing page you should read twice

Underneath the plan, Sellozo's pricing page runs a comparison headed along the lines of "compare to percentage of spend pricing", showing costs of roughly a thousand, fifteen hundred and twenty-five hundred dollars a month.

Those are not Sellozo prices, and they are not tiers you can buy. They are an illustration of what an agency charging two, three or five percent would cost on a fifty-thousand-dollar monthly ad budget, set beside Sellozo's flat fee. Read quickly — or lifted into a comparison post by someone reading quickly — it looks like a price ladder, and we have seen those figures repeated as though it were one. It is a rhetorical device, and a common one. Just do not budget from it.

The underlying argument is fair on its own terms, and it deserves a straight answer from us rather than a dodge: we charge a percentage of ad spend and we are not going to pretend that is costless. At high spend a flat fee is cheaper on paper, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling. Our percentage is published, and it is capped, which is the part that decides how the two curves cross. The honest instruction is to run both formulas at your own spend, with the cap included, and see which line you prefer.

Flat fee versus percentage, done honestly

What the paper comparison misses is what the fee buys, in either direction. A flat software fee buys you a tool that waits for you to open it. Nobody is reviewing your search terms on the Tuesday you spend in a warehouse. If your bottleneck is a person's attention rather than a licence, the cheaper line item is the more expensive decision — and the reverse is equally true if you have the attention and just need better instruments.

The other thing to force into the open, with any vendor and in any pricing model, is the whole formula. A rate you cannot see cannot be compared: on a six-figure monthly budget, a single undisclosed point of fee is a five-figure annual sum, and it will not appear on the page you are comparing. Sellozo's answer to that is a flat number with no percentage attached, which is a clean answer. Ours is a percentage that is published and capped. Both are answerable; a shrug is not.

Work out which one you are — attention-limited or instrument-limited — before you compare prices. It is the only comparison that actually decides anything.

Which one you should actually pick

Sellozo suits a hands-on seller who wants a clear visual builder and a flat fee that stays put as they grow — Campaign Studio is a genuine strength, and a published plan with a 30-day trial makes it cheap to test. It suits nobody who needs the work done rather than the interface improved. Dr. PPC is the other purchase: the execution, not the console.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

How much does Sellozo cost?

Its pricing page lists a single flat plan, the Sellozo Platform, at $250 a month for one marketplace, with a slider taking you up to twenty. Billing is month-to-month after a 30-day free trial, users are unlimited, and no percentage of ad spend is charged.

What are the larger figures on Sellozo's pricing page?

They are an illustration, not a price list. The page compares its flat fee against what an agency charging a percentage of spend would cost on a sample monthly budget, and those comparison figures get mistaken for Sellozo tiers in third-party posts. The plan you can actually buy is the flat one.

Is Sellozo still actively developed?

It is still marketed and sold, and its pricing has been simplified recently rather than left to drift. Reporting puts it inside Netrush since October 2021. Ask what shipped in the last two quarters and for references from customers onboarded in the last year — reasonable questions to ask any vendor inside a larger group.

What is Campaign Studio?

A visual, drag-and-drop interface for building and connecting campaigns. It is the feature users single out most often, and it is genuinely useful for seeing account structure as a diagram instead of a long list — particularly on accounts that have been through several owners.

Does Sellozo charge a percentage of ad spend?

No — its pricing page states a flat rate with no percentage of ad spend and no hidden fees. For a high-spending seller that is a real advantage, because the software line stops moving as the budget grows. Confirm what is included at your marketplace count, and whether any consultation or managed time is billed separately.

Who is Sellozo best for?

A seller who wants to hold the controls, values a visual campaign builder, and prefers a fee that does not move as spend grows. It is a weaker fit for anyone who needs the weekly execution done for them rather than made easier.
