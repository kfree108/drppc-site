What DataHawk's pricing page actually contains

One sentence and a button. The page states that custom pricing is available with annual plans tailored to your business, and the call to action is Book a Demo. There is no tier table, no starting-from figure, no free plan, no self-serve signup and no monthly option presented. Professional services are noted separately as a paid add-on, again with no figure attached.

That is a finding rather than an oversight, and it is a normal one. Data platforms are sold on sales volume, marketplace coverage and how much you track, so any single published figure would be wrong for most buyers — plenty of serious companies in this category price the same way. What it does mean in practice is that your number gets shaped by what they learn about you on the call, so it is worth deciding what you want connected before you dial in. It is also the reason you are reading a third-party page instead of theirs.

The two-part structure, and which part surprises people

Third-party summaries and DataHawk's own documentation consistently describe a quote built from two components. We could not see either on the pricing page today, so treat this as the shape to confirm rather than a fact to budget from.

  • A platform fee, scaled to your Amazon sales volume. It covers the software itself — collection, storage, the managed database, analytics, integrations, anomaly detection.
  • A credits fee, metered against what you choose to track. The conversion widely reported is one credit per product, one credit per keyword, and fifty credits per category.

The platform fee is the number people negotiate. The credits fee is the number that grows quietly. Keyword tracking is where it happens: a catalogue of 400 ASINs is 400 credits, but the keyword set you actually want to watch across those ASINs is a far larger number, and category tracking at fifty credits each adds up in a hurry once someone on the team develops a taste for it.

Ask for the credit allowance in the contract, the overage rate, and whether unused credits roll. Ask what a mid-term top-up costs, because that price gets set when you have the least bargaining power.

Annual-only is the term that deserves attention

The page says annual plans. That is a twelve-month commitment to a tool you will have evaluated across a demo and, if you push for it, a trial. It is not unusual, and for a data platform it is defensible — the value builds as history accumulates, and a one-month trial genuinely understates it.

But it changes the questions. Get the renewal uplift in writing. Get an exit clause if data coverage for a marketplace you rely on is discontinued. And establish who owns the historical data if you leave, and in what format you get it — for a warehousing product that is not a small point.

Five questions worth having answered before the second call: what is the platform fee at my current sales volume and at twice it; how many credits are included; what does an extra credit cost; what is the renewal uplift; and can I export my full history on exit.

Where DataHawk is genuinely the better buy

We should be straight about this. DataHawk is built for people who want the raw material, not a verdict. If you have an analyst who would rather write SQL against clean Amazon data and pipe it into your own warehouse and BI stack, DataHawk is a better purchase than our software and probably better than most of what it competes with. Multi-marketplace coverage, deep historical retention and export-first design are the point of it.

It is a poor purchase for a small team with no analyst. Data platforms of this kind reward organisations that already know which question they are asking. Buying one to find out what your problem is tends to produce a very expensive dashboard nobody opens by March.

And if the reason you are shopping for analytics is that you keep running out of stock and want to see it coming, that is a supply chain problem, not a reporting one — Dr. Stock is built for it.

What to do with this

Before you compare subscription prices, pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders. On the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Whatever you buy — a seat, a service, or nothing — that number is the one it has to move, and a cheaper tool nobody has time to drive will not move it.

Common questions

How much does DataHawk cost?

DataHawk does not publish a figure and we will not invent one. Its pricing page offers custom annual pricing and a demo booking. Any specific number you find on a comparison site is either an old quote from one customer or a guess, and neither should go in your budget.

How do DataHawk credits work?

Credits meter what you track. The conversion widely reported is one credit per product, one credit per keyword and fifty credits per category. Confirm the included allowance, the overage rate and whether credits roll over, because keyword and category tracking is where the bill grows after the first quarter.

Is there a free trial or a free plan?

No free plan is advertised. A trial, if you get one, will come out of the sales conversation rather than a signup form. Ask for it against your own catalogue and your own keyword set, not a demo account.

Is DataHawk worth it for a smaller seller?

Usually not. It is built for organisations that already have an analyst and a specific question. A smaller team is better served by a profitability tool plus something that acts on the advertising, rather than an annual contract for a data platform nobody has time to query.

What should I ask on the DataHawk demo call?

Five things: the platform fee at your current sales volume and at double it, the included credit allowance, the cost of an additional credit, the renewal uplift, and whether you can export your full history if you leave.
