Which SellerApp are you being sold?

Most reviews of SellerApp review one product and do not tell you which. That is the first thing to fix, because the company sells three quite different things on the same pricing page and the right verdict depends entirely on which one you are buying.

Self-serve software. A Freemium plan at $0 with no credit card required, a Pro plan starting at $99 per month, and a Smart Plan with Automation. This is the product most reviews are actually about.

Managed services. Listing optimisation and creation starting at $200 per parent ASIN, an AMS Growth Plan starting at $750 per month with account management, weekly updates and a monthly planning session, and an AMS Scale plan quoted on request. This is a service business, not software, and reviews of the dashboard tell you nothing about it.

An agency arrangement. Described on the page as a starting sum of $300 in addition to a percentage of Amazon ad spend, in a band of 0.5% to 2.5%.

If you read a glowing review of the keyword tools and then buy the managed service, you have bought something nobody in that review evaluated. Establish which product a review is describing before you weigh it — and that goes for reviews of every vendor in this category, ours included.

The most interesting number on their pricing page is about us

SellerApp publishes an agency arrangement of $300 plus a percentage of Amazon ad spend ranging between 0.5% and 2.5%. Dr. PPC is $300 a month plus 3% of ad spend. Those are, structurally, the same product of arithmetic — a fixed monthly line plus a share of what you spend — and the fixed line is the same figure to the dollar.

We are pointing that out rather than hoping you do not notice it, for two reasons.

The first is that it settles an argument that comes up constantly. Percentage-of-spend pricing is not an unusual or aggressive model in Amazon advertising; it is what the market has converged on. Helium 10, the largest company in the seller-software category, charges a 2% management fee on PPC spend managed through Helium 10 Ads. Teikametrics adds a percentage above a spend threshold. Perpetua's growth tier is a fixed fee plus an unstated percentage. When independent competitors land on the same shape, the shape is the market, not a trick.

The second is that their published band tops out below our rate. At 2.5% versus 3%, a brand comparing headline percentages will find SellerApp's lower, and that is a real difference we are not going to explain away. The things worth weighing against it are that our percentage is a single published figure rather than a range you negotiate into, that our total is capped so it stops growing, that the first 30 days are free, and that our fee covers the account being operated rather than the software being licensed. Whether that trade is worth half a percentage point is your call, not ours.

Read the asterisk on the Smart Plan

The self-serve tiers reward a careful read. The Smart Plan with Automation is presented with two figures — a headline starting at $149 and a $250 figure alongside it — and a footnote reading "Discounted pricing is for first 3 months."

That is disclosed, plainly, on the page. It is not hidden and this is not a complaint. But it changes the number you should budget with: the rate you sign up at is a promotional rate with a stated expiry, and the standing rate is the higher of the two figures. If you are comparing SellerApp against another platform in a spreadsheet, put the standing rate in the spreadsheet, not the promotional one, or you will be comparing month two against someone else's month twenty-four.

An annual option is also offered with a stated saving on subscription fees; check the exact figure and which tab you are reading on the live page before you rely on it. That advice is not specific to SellerApp — reading the wrong side of a billing toggle is the most common error in published pricing comparisons in this category, and it is one we have made ourselves and had to correct.

One genuinely good thing on the page that deserves calling out: "No minimum subscription duration required." That is rarer than it should be. Several platforms in this market run one-year terms that renew automatically and require sixty days' written notice to stop. A vendor that says plainly there is no minimum is removing a real risk from your side of the deal, and they should get credit for it.

Why this review quotes no star ratings

You will find SellerApp review scores quoted all over the search results. We are not going to add to them, and the reason is worth explaining because it applies to every review you read about this category.

The major review platforms block automated reading. A rating quoted from a search snippet is a rating as it stood whenever that snippet was cached, which may be years ago and may not match the live page — in our own checking, snippet figures have contradicted live pages more than once. Repeating a number we cannot see is how a page becomes confidently wrong, and a confidently wrong number about a competitor is the most expensive mistake a page like this can make.

What we can say usefully instead: date the review, then verify on a reference call. Ask the vendor for two customers who have been live in the last six months, in roughly your category and at roughly your spend, and ask those customers about support responsiveness and data accuracy specifically. That takes an afternoon and is worth more than every aggregate score on the internet. Apply the same standard to us — Dr. PPC is a young product, and any review of it describes a particular month.

What a platform this broad is good at, and what it cannot be

The free tier is a real thing and a generous one. For a seller starting out, being able to look at keyword and listing data without a credit card removes a genuine barrier, and SellerApp deserves credit for offering it when most of the category will not.

The keyword and listing work is where the platform is strongest. Search-term discovery, listing quality analysis and the multi-marketplace coverage are the parts that earn the subscription, and if that is the job you are hiring it for, it does the job.

What follows structurally from that breadth — software, managed services and an agency arrangement under one roof, across several marketplaces — is that no single module can be as deep as a specialist tool built for that module alone. That is not a flaw, it is a design choice, and it is the same choice every suite in this market makes. The practical version of it: if you need one platform that covers many jobs adequately, breadth is what you want. If you need the single best keyword research tool on the market, buy the single best keyword research tool.

The harder limit is one no platform in this category escapes. Software gives you more data; it does not give you fewer decisions. A better dashboard usually means more things you can now see are wrong, all of which still require someone to act.

What more data does not solve

Here is that problem with real numbers on it, from an account published with the brand's permission. Walkize ran $267,131 of Amazon ad spend from December 2025 to August 2026 against $601,614 in attributed sales, across 30,921 unique search terms and 244,510 clicks at a $1.09 blended cost-per-click.

Thirty thousand search terms is more than any human reviews weekly. It is also exactly the kind of data every platform in this category will happily show you, sorted and filtered and charted. But the sorting is not the work.

Two findings from that data set show why. 89,045 individual search terms took money and returned no orders, consuming $89,885 — 33.6% of every dollar spent. And separately, the top 1% of search terms drove 75.5% of all sales.

Neither of those is a reporting problem. The waste was visible in the data the whole time; nobody had the hours to act on eighty-nine thousand individual judgements. And the concentration finding means the most valuable work in that account was not trimming the tail at all — it was defending a few hundred terms that carried three quarters of the revenue. Automation rules do not solve this either, because to catch 89,045 losing terms with rules you would have to have known to write a rule for each one, and the tail is long precisely because it is unrepeatable.

This is the gap between a platform and an operator, and it is the gap every honest review of every tool in this category should name.

Which one you should actually pick

SellerApp suits multi-marketplace sellers doing serious keyword and listing work, and its free tier and stated lack of a minimum subscription duration make it unusually low-risk to try. It suits you less well if you want to be told what to do rather than shown what is happening, since the platform hands you data and options. Dr. PPC suits brands who would rather buy the decision than the dashboard, at a published rate with the work included.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

Is SellerApp free?

There is a genuine Freemium plan at $0 with no credit card required, alongside paid tiers. It is a real free plan rather than a trial that expires, which is unusual in this category and a legitimate reason to start there. Expect the free tier to cover looking rather than automating — the automation features sit on the paid Smart Plan.

How much does SellerApp cost?

The Pro plan starts at $99 per month and the Smart Plan with Automation shows a headline starting at $149 against a $250 figure, footnoted as discounted pricing for the first three months. Managed services are separate: listing work from $200 per parent ASIN and an AMS Growth Plan from $750 per month. Budget with the standing rate rather than the promotional one.

Does SellerApp charge a percentage of ad spend?

On its published agency arrangement, yes — described as a starting sum of $300 plus a percentage of Amazon ad spend in a 0.5% to 2.5% band. The self-serve seller plans are flat subscriptions. This is worth knowing because it is nearly the same structure Dr. PPC uses at $300 plus 3%, which tells you the model is a market norm rather than an outlier.

Is SellerApp good for PPC management?

It gives you the data and, on the automation tier, rules to act on it, which is a real step up from working in Seller Central alone. What it does not do is make the judgement calls — someone on your side still sets the rules, reviews the outcomes and handles everything the rules did not anticipate. If that person exists and has the time, it is a capable platform. If they do not, more data will not close the gap.
