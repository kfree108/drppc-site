Helium 10 review: the tier you end up on is decided by seats, not features

Updated 2026-08-21 · 2812 words · Written against what currently ranked for “helium 10 review”
The short answer

Helium 10 is the most complete Amazon seller suite on the market and its research tools have no real equivalent elsewhere. The catch most reviews miss is that your tier is usually forced by seat and connected-account limits rather than by features, and that advertising sits behind Diamond with a 2% fee on managed spend.

The honest short version

Helium 10 is very good, widely used, and the default answer for a reason. If you sell on Amazon and you need to find out what a keyword is worth, what a competitor is doing, or whether a product idea has demand, its research tools are the strongest generally available option and nothing in our own suite replaces them. We will say that at the top rather than bury it, because a review that spends 2,000 words setting up a pivot to the author's own product is not a review.

What a review owes you beyond that is the parts that are genuinely hard to work out before you buy. There are three, and they are all things we had to read carefully rather than recall:

  • Which tier you will actually end up on, which is usually not decided by the feature you were shopping for.
  • What the advertising module is now called and what it costs, because both changed and most published reviews predate the change.
  • What the subscription does not include, which is the same thing every software subscription in this category does not include: somebody to do the work.

The tier is chosen for you by seats and connected accounts

Here is the finding that changed how we read the plan grid, and we have not seen it made anywhere else.

Most reviews compare the tiers on tool access — which searches you get, how many keyword lookups, whether a particular module is unlocked. In practice, for anyone past their first product, the binding constraint is not a tool at all. It is these two numbers, taken from the pricing page:

  • Platinum — 1 additional user account, 2 connected Amazon accounts.
  • Diamond — 5 additional users, 10 connected Amazon accounts.
  • Enterprise — 10+ users, 15+ seller accounts.

Two connected accounts is not many. A brand with a North American seller account and a European one has used both. An agency with three clients has run out. A seller who has a legacy account plus the entity the business actually trades through has run out. In every one of those cases you move to Diamond for reasons that have nothing to do with capability — and on annual billing that step is $99/month to $279/month, or $2,160 a year for administrative headroom.

Now turn it around, because the arithmetic is much friendlier from the other end. Per connected account, Platinum works out at $49.50/month and Diamond at $27.90/month on annual billing. Diamond is 44% cheaper per account than Platinum. For an agency, an aggregator, or anyone running several seller entities, the tier everyone describes as the expensive one is the cheaper one on the only unit that matters to them.

The practical advice is unglamorous: count your seats and your seller accounts before you compare a single feature. On most shortlists that one count settles the plan, and the feature comparison you were about to do turns out to have been decorative.

The advertising module: what it is called now, and what it costs

If you are reading this because you want Helium 10 to run your ads, three things need updating from almost every review in circulation.

First, the name. The advertising product is Helium 10 Ads. The word Adtomic does not appear on the advertising product page. The lineage is worth knowing because it explains the tool's design: Helium 10 acquired Prestozon, fused it with its own advertising tool, and the company's own announcement describes the result as "the fusion between two great pieces of Amazon advertising software, Helium 10 ADS and Prestozon". That post was published in May 2021 and carries a modified date of 19 May 2026 — the rename was applied to it retroactively, which is a neat illustration of how quickly a product's name can outrun the articles written about it.

Second, the gate. Helium 10's pricing page answers the question directly: "Helium 10 Ads is available in Diamond plans." If you were planning to buy Platinum for the research tools and also run ads inside it, that is not the box those tools are in.

Third, the fee. Attached to the Diamond tier on the same page is this sentence: "Diamond plan customers incur a 2% management fee on PPC spend managed through Helium 10 Ads." It is published plainly, which is more than several vendors in this category manage, and it is quoted by almost nobody. It also means the subscription stops being the price the moment ads are running — at $50,000 of monthly managed spend the fee is $1,000, several times the Diamond line itself. We could find no published ceiling on it and no threshold below which it does not apply.

We are not going to make an argument out of the model. Dr. PPC charges 3% of ad spend on top of $300 a month, so attacking percentage pricing would be absurd. The two questions worth asking about any percentage are what it is charged on — total account spend or only managed spend — and whether it is capped. Ours is capped at $2,500 a month. Ask them, and ask us.

What the tools are genuinely excellent at

The research side is the reason Helium 10 has the market position it has, and no honest review understates it.

The keyword and competitor research tools do something that is hard to replicate: they turn Amazon's search surface into a queryable dataset, quickly, for a fee that is trivial next to the decisions it informs. Working out which terms a competing ASIN ranks for, what a category's demand actually looks like, or whether a listing is leaving obvious keyword coverage on the table are all jobs that take minutes there and hours anywhere else. If you launch products, that alone justifies the subscription.

The advertising module, on its own terms, is a mature rules engine with real lineage. Its own product page describes tracking every advertising decision in one simplified view including total advertising cost of sale, automating bid strategies, and turning "hours of manual search term placements into minutes of easy sorting and filtering". That is an accurate description of what it does well. Sorting, filtering and acting on a search term report at speed is genuinely valuable, and Diamond adds rules-based ad automation and dayparting.

The multi-user and multi-account structure at Diamond is also better than most competitors of similar price, which is the flip side of the seat analysis above.

Where we would set expectations carefully is the breadth. A suite this large will always contain more tools than any one seller opens. That is not a flaw — it is what a suite is — but it does mean the useful question when comparing plans is not "how many tools" but "which three will I open every week, and are they in this tier". Answer that honestly and the plan choice usually gets simpler and cheaper.

The wall every rules engine hits

This is the structural limit, and it applies to every tool in the category including the ones we compete with and the automations inside our own product. A rules engine can only act on a problem someone has already noticed.

Consider what that means at real volume. Across the 47 brands we manage, Amazon delivered clicks on 3,086,624 distinct search terms from 1 May 2026 onward, against $10,243,379 of search spend. That is the population a rules engine is being asked to govern, and none of it was chosen by a person.

A well-configured rules engine handles most of that beautifully. Bids move, budgets shift, obvious losers get negated. What it cannot do is tell you that a rule you never wrote is the one you needed — that a term is cheap and converting but capped by budget in a campaign you set up eleven months ago, or that two campaigns are bidding against each other on the same phrase. Those are noticing problems, not execution problems, and the amount of noticing an account needs scales with its complexity rather than with its spend.

That is the honest boundary of what a $279 subscription buys. It buys capability and speed. It does not buy attention, and attention is the input that is actually scarce.

Which is why the fair comparison is never "$279 against $300 plus 3%". It is "$279 plus somebody's weekly hours" against a single line item with the hours inside it. If the person doing those hours is you and you enjoy it, Helium 10 wins that comparison comfortably and we would tell you so on a call.

Read the contract, not the feature grid

Reviews compare features. Contracts decide risk, and almost nobody reads them before signing.

Helium 10's own commitment question is straightforward but worth being deliberate about: the discount you see on Platinum and Diamond is attached to annual billing, so taking $279 instead of $359 means committing for a year. That is a normal trade and often a good one. It is still a trade, and it is the moment to ask what happens if you need to downgrade in month four, or if your seller-account count changes.

For a sense of how much heavier these terms can get elsewhere, the clearest published example in the category belongs to Quartile, whose terms set a one-year initial term that automatically renews for successive one-year periods, require written notice of non-renewal at least sixty days before expiry, make fees due in advance and non-cancellable and non-refundable, and allow termination from inside the account only during an initial sixty-day evaluation window. Publishing that openly is to their credit; the point is that a one-year auto-renewing term with sixty days' notice leaves you about 305 days a year in which leaving is actually an option.

The checklist we would take to any vendor here, ours included:

  • Term and renewal. Month-to-month, or annual with automatic renewal?
  • Notice. How many days, in what form, and diary it the week you sign.
  • Fee changes. Ask for written notice of any change, from anybody including us.
  • Percentage basis. Total spend or managed spend, and is it capped.
  • Seats and accounts. What happens mid-term when you need the eleventh connected account.
  • Data portability. On exit, do your keyword lists, negative lists and historical reports leave with you?

Who should buy it, who should not, and where we fit

Buy Helium 10 if you launch or research products regularly; if one or two people will genuinely sit in it each week; if two connected accounts and two users cover you, in which case Platinum at $99/month on annual billing is one of the better deals in Amazon software; or if you run many seller accounts, in which case Diamond's per-account economics are strong.

Look elsewhere if your only requirement is automated bidding and you have no interest in research — a narrower tool priced on catalogue size, such as Scale Insights' ladder from $78/month billed monthly, will cost less for that single job. And if nobody in your business is going to open the software on a Tuesday, no tier of any suite is the right purchase.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — across the 47 brands above that runs at 48.5% of all search spend. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

Is Helium 10 worth it in 2026?

For research, yes, and it is close to unarguable — the keyword and competitor tools do a job that would otherwise cost far more in time. For advertising specifically the answer depends on whether someone will operate it weekly. Note also that the plan you end up on is often decided by seat and connected-account limits rather than by tools: Platinum allows 1 additional user and 2 connected Amazon accounts, Diamond allows 5 and 10.

Which Helium 10 plan includes ads?

Diamond. Helium 10's pricing page states that Helium 10 Ads is available in Diamond plans, and that Diamond customers incur a 2% management fee on PPC spend managed through Helium 10 Ads. So the advertising decision is really a two-part one: the step from Platinum at $99/month on annual billing to Diamond at $279, plus a variable fee that grows with your spend and has no published ceiling that we could find.

What happened to Adtomic?

It is now called Helium 10 Ads, and the older name no longer appears on the advertising product page. The lineage runs through Prestozon, which Helium 10 acquired and fused with its own advertising tool. This matters when reading reviews: an article about Adtomic, or one about Prestozon, is describing an earlier version of the same product under a different name, and any pricing in it should be checked against the current page rather than trusted.

Is Helium 10 cheaper than Dr. PPC?

On the invoice, at most spend levels, yes. Helium 10 sells software you operate; Dr. PPC is $300/month plus 3% of ad spend, capped at $2,500/month, with the daily work included and human operators supervising an AI agent. The comparison only becomes like-for-like once you add the cost of whoever will sit in the software every week — which is a figure only you can supply, and the reason we would rather you did the arithmetic than took our word for it.

Can I use Helium 10 and Dr. PPC together?

Yes, and a fair number of sellers should. They solve different problems: Helium 10 is unmatched for keyword and competitor research and product discovery, while Dr. PPC runs the advertising day to day. Keeping Platinum for research at $99/month on annual billing and handing the ads to us is a perfectly coherent stack, and it avoids paying the Diamond step for a module you would not be operating.
